# Org, people, governance and regulation in an AI-driven SDLC migration

Research brief for Part III (Ch 13–17). Compiled 2026-10-15. The session's web-search budget ran out partway through, so several leads below were found but not verified against a primary source. They are listed under "Unverified leads" and must not be cited as fact until checked.

## Key findings

- **Entry-level hiring is where the labour-market effect shows up, and it appears as fewer hires, not more layoffs.** Stanford DEL (Aug 2026 revision) puts employment of 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations 19% below the counterfactual. Harvard résumé data shows junior employment about 9% lower in GenAI-adopting firms after six quarters. Both studies say the mechanism is reduced hiring. The Yale Budget Lab finds no economy-wide disruption, and the Stanford authors agree on that point. → Ch 16.
- **The junior pipeline problem is about skills as well as jobs.** In Anthropic's RCT, AI-assisted juniors scored 17 points lower on a mastery quiz, with the biggest gap in debugging, and were not significantly faster. Anthropic's internal study reports that juniors ask seniors fewer questions. Both point to a mentorship gap that an AI-driven SDLC has to design for. → Ch 16.
- **Giving people licences does not produce adoption.** In three field RCTs covering 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm, about 30–40% of engineers never tried Copilot, even though it was free and in-IDE. Short-tenure and junior developers adopted more, kept using it longer and gained more. → Ch 14, Ch 15.
- **A clear, communicated AI policy is a measured capability, not paperwork.** DORA's 2025 AI Capabilities Model lists "clear and communicated AI stance" as one of seven capabilities that amplify AI's benefit. DORA explicitly says the content of the stance matters less than its clarity. → Ch 15.
- **AI amplifies whatever the organisation already is.** DORA 2024: a 25% rise in AI adoption was associated with an estimated −1.5% delivery throughput and −7.2% delivery stability, even as documentation, code quality and review speed rose. DORA 2025 frames AI as "a mirror and a multiplier". Faros telemetry finds team-level gains that "do not scale when aggregated". → Ch 17, Ch 13.
- **Measurement has to pair speed with quality and keep AI metrics out of individual performance reviews.** DX's AI Measurement Framework (utilisation / impact / cost) warns that code-generation volume is especially gameable and advises not to feed AI metrics into individual evaluation. METR's RCT showed developers believed AI sped them up by 20% when it slowed them by 19%, so self-report alone is unsafe. → Ch 17.
- **Roles are shifting toward supervising agents.** Anthropic engineers describe themselves as "manager[s] of AI agents". One estimated their work moving "70%+ to being a code reviewer/reviser". Microsoft's Work Trend Index coins "agent boss" and the "human-agent ratio". Hard data on new formal job titles is thin (vendor surveys only). → Ch 16.
- **Open-source governance has split into three camps, and they give the book ready-made policy templates.** The camps are ban (Gentoo, QEMU), presumed-tainted unless core approves (NetBSD), and allow with disclosure and human accountability (Linux kernel's `Assisted-by:` + human-only `Signed-off-by`; Fedora). → Ch 15, appendix templates.
- **Regulation affecting software teams is mostly about literacy, security and documentation, not banning coding assistants.** EU AI Act Article 4 (AI literacy) has applied since 2 Feb 2025. The Digital Omnibus (in force 27 Jul 2026) moved Annex III high-risk duties to 2 Dec 2027 and Annex I to 2 Aug 2028. The EU Cyber Resilience Act's vulnerability reporting applies from 11 Sep 2026 and its main obligations from 11 Dec 2027. NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 are voluntary frameworks. → Ch 15.
- **IP-indemnity terms are volatile.** Microsoft's Customer Copyright Commitment no longer requires GitHub's Duplicate Detection filter as of 3 April 2026. Governance checklists written in 2024 are already wrong on this point. → Ch 15 (volatile fact, needs an "as of" marker).

## Claims

1. **Young workers in AI-exposed jobs are 19% below the counterfactual.** · "employment of young workers (ages 22–25) in AI-exposed occupations now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers; experienced workers show no comparable gap." · https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf (abstract read from the PDF) and https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ · 2026-08-12 · independent academic study (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, Stanford DEL) using ADP payroll data · high · Ch 16
2. **The gap has widened across revisions: 13% (Aug 2025) → 16% (Nov 2025) → 19% (Aug 2026).** · The abstract says the gap "has widened steadily since we first documented it in August 2025". The DEL news page gives 15% "at the July 2025 data vintage". · same sources. The 13%/16% figures come from secondary coverage of the earlier versions. · 2025-08 → 2026-08 · independent academic · medium (13/16 secondary; 15 vs 13 discrepancy reflects re-estimation on new data) · Ch 16
3. **No economy-wide displacement.** · "We find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement." Average ADP employment rose about 6% between Nov 2022 and Jun 2026; the most exposed quintile grew about 4%. · Canaries Aug 2026 PDF, Fact 1 · 2026-08 · independent academic · high · Ch 16
4. **The mechanism is hiring, not firing.** · The effect "operates primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations." · Canaries Aug 2026 PDF · 2026-08 · independent · high · Ch 16

6. **The divergence is not specific to tech firms.** · It "persists when excluding technology firms and computer occupations". ## Contradictions & debates

- **Is AI cutting junior jobs?**
  - Stanford DEL (−19% vs counterfactual) and Harvard (−9% at adopters) say yes, for exposed roles, via hiring.
  - Yale Budget Lab (Gimbel et al., Oct 2025) finds "no discernible disruption" economy-wide and attributes graduate unemployment mostly to macro factors. Gimbel told Fortune in Feb 2026 that interest rates and the post-pandemic hiring correction explain more. These are secondary reports; the primary page failed to load.
  - SignalFire, despite reporting the steepest new-grad drop, says the "real story is more nuanced".
  - The Stanford authors themselves note that the effect attenuates with education controls, shows some pre-trends, and is stronger in ADP than in CPS/ACS.
  - Book stance: real but contested, descriptive not causal, concentrated in hiring.
- **Mandates vs. voluntary adoption.**
  - The Cui et al. data (30–40% never try a free tool) supports the case for active enablement or mandates.
  - DX advises against "top-down mandates and individual evaluation".
  - DORA says a clear stance helps whatever its content, which suggests the clarity of a mandate may matter more than its force.
  - Shopify and Coinbase show mandates tied to performance reviews or firing, but no published outcomes. There is no primary evidence that mandates outperform enablement.
- **Self-reported vs. measured productivity.**
  - DORA 2025 (>80% feel more productive), Anthropic (+50% self-reported) and Atlassian (68% save more than 10 hours) all rest on self-report.
  - METR (19% slower while believing 20% faster), DORA 2024 (−1.5% throughput, −7.2% stability) and Faros (no company-level gain) all disagree.
  - METR says its own result is now out of date, which is a caveat on the caveat.
- **Juniors: who benefits?**
  - Cui et al. find juniors gain most in output.
  - Anthropic's RCT finds juniors learn less.
  - Both can be true: short-term output versus long-term skill formation. This is the core tension for Ch 16.
- **Open-source policy split.**
  - Gentoo and QEMU ban AI contributions.
  - NetBSD presumes AI code is tainted and requires core approval.
  - The Linux kernel and Fedora allow it with disclosure.
  - Copyright risk is the main stated reason for bans. Microsoft dropping the filter requirement from its indemnity (Apr 2026) shows vendors' own risk assessment has moved.
- **Linux kernel `Assisted-by` format.** The v7.2 docs say `AGENT_NAME:MODEL_VERSION`; next says `LLM`. Provenance granularity is itself being debated (naming the model versus naming the category).

## Open questions for the book

1. Ch 16 needs a defensible stance on juniors. If short-term output gains (Cui) and long-term skill loss (Anthropic RCT) are both true, what does a "junior track" at L2/L3 look like? Candidates are mandatory comprehension modes, review-first apprenticeships and AI-off katas. Can we find any company that measured junior ramp-up time under AI?
2. Is there any primary case of an AI-champions or enablement programme with before/after adoption numbers that is not vendor-run? If not, Ch 15 should say so plainly.
3. Mandate vs. enablement: is there any controlled comparison? If none exists, the book should present DORA's "clear stance" finding as the evidence-backed middle path.
4. For governance templates, should the book recommend the kernel-style `Assisted-by:` trailer plus human-only sign-off as the default provenance practice? How does that interact with agent-authored PRs at L3/L4, where no human wrote the diff?
5. EU AI Act: do internal coding agents ever make a company a "provider" or a high-risk deployer? Likely not, but Article 4 literacy applies to any deployer. Ch 15 needs a lawyer-checked paragraph. The CRA probably matters more to software teams than the AI Act; confirm with a primary legal source.
6. Measurement: which single "north-star" set should Ch 17 recommend? An option is DORA four keys plus rework, plus DX utilisation and cost, plus a quality guardrail. How should rollback criteria be stated, given DORA 2024's stability drop?
7. The "% of code written by AI" metric (Google, Microsoft, Coinbase executives quote it) has no standard definition. Should the book explicitly warn against it as a success metric? DX implies it is gameable.
