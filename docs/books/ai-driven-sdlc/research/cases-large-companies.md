# Large-company cases: organisations moving their SDLC to AI, with measured outcomes

Research brief for *From SDLC to AI-Driven SDLC: A Migration Handbook*. Compiled 2026-10-15.
Maturity levels: L0 Manual · L1 Assisted · L2 Delegated · L3 Supervised-autonomous · L4 Autonomous.
Phases: requirements, design, coding, review, testing, CI/CD, operations, maintenance.

**Evidence grades used below**
- **A**: peer-reviewed or arXiv paper with a stated method and denominators (still usually authored by company staff).
- **B**: company engineering blog with concrete figures and some stated method.
- **C**: executive statement (earnings call, LinkedIn/X post, interview) with a figure and no method.
- **D**: policy or memo with no outcome measurement, or a figure known only through press.

None of the corporate cases below is independently audited. The only results measured by people outside the company's own staff are the academic co-authors on the Cui et al. field experiments and Faros (a vendor) on pooled telemetry.

**Search budget note:** the session's WebSearch budget ran out partway through. A few leads (Goldman primary interview, Meta's ACH blog post, Shopify X post text, Duolingo LinkedIn text) are therefore backed only by press. Those claims are marked as secondary.

## Key findings

1. **The best-evidenced large-company wins are all in maintenance and migrations.** They are bounded, mechanically verifiable changes: Google's LLM migrations, Airbnb's Enzyme→RTL move, Amazon's Java 17 upgrades and Spotify's Honk fleet changes. Each has a deterministic check (compiler, tests, lint) that closes the loop. *Serves Ch 3, Ch 12; supports the Ch 1 thesis that verification, not generation, gates autonomy.*
2. **In migration case after case, review becomes the bottleneck.** Google throttled JUnit CLs per week so reviewers were not overwhelmed. Spotify said PR review, not generation, became the constraint. Faros saw PR review time rise 91% on high-AI teams. *Serves Ch 1, Ch 8.*
3. **The headline "time saved" figures are almost always estimates against a counterfactual nobody ran.** Examples: Amazon's 4,500 developer-years, Airbnb's "1.5 years by hand", and Google's ~50% (developer-reported). The book should present them as self-reported estimates, not measurements. *Serves Ch 3, Ch 4.*
4. **"% of code written by AI" is the most-quoted and least-defined metric.** Google says >25% "generated by AI, then reviewed and accepted by engineers". Microsoft says "20 to 30 percent… in some of our projects". Coinbase's figure went from ~40% to "95–100% written by or with LLMs". None of these defines its denominator, and the numbers say nothing about outcomes. *Serves Ch 4, Ch 17 (metric anti-pattern).*
5. **The strongest causal evidence is randomized trials of L1 autocomplete, not agents.** Cui et al. pooled RCTs at Microsoft, Accenture and a Fortune 100 firm: +26.08% completed tasks (SE 10.3%), 4,867 developers. Google's internal RCT found ~21% faster on one task with a "large" confidence interval. No comparable RCT exists for L2–L4 agent deployment at a large company. *Serves Ch 4, Ch 7.*
6. **Gains for individuals do not automatically show up at the organisation level.** Faros's telemetry from 10,000+ developers found +21% tasks and +98% PRs merged on high-adoption teams. It found no significant correlation with company-level throughput, DORA metrics or quality. *Serves Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 17.*
7. **Review and testing agents are reaching L2–L3 with measured acceptance rates.** Uber's uReview covers 90%+ of ~65k weekly diffs, with ~75% of comments rated useful and 65% addressed. Google's ML-suggested edits resolve 7.5% of reviewer comments. Meta's TestGen-LLM had 73% of recommendations accepted, and Meta's ACH had 73% of its tests accepted. *Serves Ch 8, Ch 9.*
8. **Stripe and Spotify show L3-style "agent opens the PR, human reviews" at volume.** Stripe reports 1,000+ merged PRs/week with no human-written code, every one human-reviewed. Spotify reports 1,500+ merged agent PRs and 60–90% time savings on measured migrations. Neither reports failure or revert rates. *Serves Ch 3, Ch 7, Ch 12.*
9. **Mandates are not outcomes.** The Shopify, Duolingo and Coinbase mandates, Amazon's reported 80% weekly Kiro-use goal and Salesforce's hiring freeze are adoption policies or headcount statements. Two produced public reversals or incidents: Duolingo's clarification, and the FT-reported AWS outage that Amazon attributes to "user error". Klarna's AI-first customer-service reversal is outside the SDLC but is the clearest public example of "cost-first AI degrades quality". *Serves Ch 3 (failures), Ch 15, Ch 16.*
10. **Granting an agent production permissions is a distinct maturity step that needs its own guardrails.** Amazon's response to the Kiro incident was to add mandatory peer review for production access, which pulled operations back from an implicit L3/L4 to L2. *Serves Ch 10, Ch 11, Ch 17.*

## Contradictions & debates

- **Amazon's own numbers don't line up.** Jassy's post says "4,500 developer-years". The AWS blog says "over 4,500 years of development work for over a thousand developers". Earnings-call coverage (Worklife, secondary) cites ~30,000 applications, while the AWS blog says "tens of thousands". The 4,500 figure appears to be 50 dev-days × application count, a counterfactual multiplication rather than measured effort. Benzinga reportedly noted early incorrect outputs (unverified).
- **"% of code written by AI" is incommensurable across firms.** Google's figure counts code "generated by AI, then reviewed and accepted". Nadella gave "20 to 30 percent" and, in the same chat, reportedly "30–40 percent" (acceptance-rate based). Coinbase's "by or with LLMs" counts assisted code. Pichai's ~10% velocity figure next to ">25–30% AI code" is itself evidence that code share does not equal productivity.
- **Individual vs. organisational gains.** RCTs (Cui et al. +26%, Google ~21%) and company blogs show task-level gains. Faros found no company-level correlation with throughput, DORA or quality, plus +91% review time and +9% bugs/developer. Both can be true; the book should present this as the "bottleneck moves" thesis of Ch 1.
- **Quality trade-offs are mostly unmeasured.** Only Google states quality explicitly as unknown ("remains to be seen"). Uber's "addressed" metric is a proxy and does not confirm correctness. Stripe and Spotify report no revert or incident rates. ANZ secondary summaries conflict on whether quality improved significantly.
- **Kiro outage attribution.** The FT (4 unnamed sources) blames the agent; Amazon blames access-control misconfiguration. Both readings support the same lesson: the permissions model is the control that matters.
- **The headcount narrative is contested by its own authors.** Salesforce froze engineering hiring on a 30% claim. Duolingo reversed its framing within weeks. Klarna reversed its AI-first support model. Later reporting (AI Magazine, secondary, unverified) says Salesforce still employs ~15,000 engineers.
- **Tool choice vs. pipeline design.** Airbnb found retries and context/file selection mattered more than prompt wording. Google combined deterministic AST tooling with LLMs. Spotify moved from a 20,000-line deterministic Maven updater to agents. The lesson is the verification harness, not the model.

## Open questions for the book

1. **Should Ch 3 grade every case visibly (A–D) so readers see evidence quality?** Recommended: yes. The spec's "case-study verification" risk argues for it.
2. **How should a vendor's claim about its own tool (Amazon Q, Microsoft reviewer) be framed?** Suggest a standard "self-reported by the vendor of the tool" tag.
3. **Is there any large-company case where an L3/L4 rollout was measured on outcomes (incidents, change failure rate, revert rate) rather than volume?** None found. This is the book's biggest evidence gap and worth stating openly.
4. **Should "% of code written by AI" appear in Ch 17 as an explicit anti-metric?** The evidence supports it.
5. **Do any companies publish revert or rollback rates for agent-authored PRs?** Stripe, Spotify and Google don't (Google only says reverts happened). A targeted follow-up search is recommended.
6. **Maturity mapping.** The migration cases suggest L3 is reachable first in maintenance because the verifier (compiler + tests) exists. Should Ch 2 make "an automated verifier exists" an explicit prerequisite for L3 in any phase?
7. **Klarna sits outside the SDLC.** Include it as a sidebar analogue, or drop it to keep the case set strictly SDLC?
8. **Volatile figures** (Coinbase %, Stripe PRs/week, Spotify pace) need "as of" markers. Stripe: as of Feb 2026. Spotify: as of Nov 2025 / Mar 2026. Coinbase: as of Jul 2026.
