# Evidence that AI shifts the SDLC bottleneck

Research brief for *From SDLC to AI-Driven SDLC*. Compiled 2026-10-15. Serves mainly Ch 1 ("When code gets cheap, the bottleneck moves"), plus Ch 4 (economics and measurement), Ch 8 (review), Ch 9 (testing), Ch 10 (CI/CD) and Ch 17 (measuring success).

Method note: DORA 2024 and 2025 figures were checked against the full report PDFs (downloaded from services.google.com and read as text). Every other claim was checked against the primary web page or paper unless marked *secondary*. Partway through, the session's web-search budget ran out. Leads that needed more searching are listed under "Unverified leads" and were not written up as claims.

## Key findings

- **The strongest evidence that the constraint moves comes from DORA, two years in a row.** In 2024 AI adoption improved process measures (code quality, review speed, documentation) but lowered delivery throughput (−1.5%) and stability (−7.2%) per 25% more adoption. In 2025 throughput turned positive and instability still rose. DORA's own explanation is larger batches that are harder to review. → Ch 1, Ch 10
- **DORA 2025 names where friction goes:** "It shifts from manual grind to deciding and verifying." This is the cleanest single quote for the chapter's thesis. → Ch 1, Ch 8, Ch 9
- **Vendor telemetry (Faros, LinearB, Jellyfish) agrees on direction: more PRs, bigger PRs, longer review queues.** Faros 2025 found +98% merged PRs and +91% review time, with no company-level gain. Faros 2026 found +441.5% median time in review and +31.3% PRs merged with no review. LinearB found agentic PRs wait 17.6 h at P75 for pickup vs 3.4 h for unassisted PRs, and merge 32.7% vs 84.4%. All of this is vendor-run and correlational. → Ch 1, Ch 4, Ch 8
- **Coding is a minority of the value stream, which caps any coding-only speedup (Amdahl's law).** Bain: writing and testing code is about 25–35% of idea-to-launch time. Atlassian: developers spend 16% of their time coding. Faros cites Amdahl's law explicitly. → Ch 1, Ch 4
- **Controlled-trial results range from strongly positive to negative, depending on task, codebase and developer familiarity.** Copilot on a greenfield HTTP server: 55.8% faster. Google enterprise task: about 21% faster. Three field RCTs: +26% completed tasks. METR, experienced developers on their own large repos with early-2025 tools: 19% *slower*. METR's late-2025 rerun is inconclusive, partly because developers refused to work without AI. → Ch 1, Ch 4
- **Perception is not measurement.** METR developers believed they were about 20% faster while measured 19% slower. Self-reported gains (DORA >80%, Atlassian 68% saving >10 h/week, Anthropic about +50%) are far larger than telemetry-based organisational gains (Google's own "about 10% engineering velocity", Bain's 10–15%). → Ch 4, Ch 17
- **Trust sits at the verification bottleneck.** Stack Overflow 2025: 46% distrust AI accuracy, only 3.1% "highly trust", and 66% are frustrated by "almost right" solutions. DORA 2025: 30% have little or no trust. Verification work is the cost that does not shrink. → Ch 1, Ch 8, Ch 9
- **Quality and maintainability signals point the wrong way, but every source is a vendor and correlational.** GitClear reports refactoring falling from 25% to under 10% of changed lines (2021→2024) and duplication up 81% (2023→2026 YTD). CodeRabbit reports AI PRs carry about 1.7× more issues. Uplevel reports +41% bugs. GitHub's own RCT finds small *quality gains*. → Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 12
- **DORA's remedy is systemic, not tool-level.** The AI Capabilities Model has seven capabilities: small batches, strong version control and rollback, quality platforms, AI-accessible internal data, and others. Value-stream management is the "force multiplier". DORA's advice is to point AI at the actual constraint, such as code review, "rather than using AI to simply generate more code that will only exacerbate the bottleneck." → Ch 1, Ch 2, Ch 15

## Claims

1. **DORA 2024: AI adoption lowered delivery throughput and stability.** "We see that the effect on delivery throughput is small, but likely negative (an estimated 1.5% reduction for every 25% increase in AI adoption). The negative impact on delivery stability is larger (an estimated 7.2% reduction for every 25% increase in AI adoption)." · https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2024_final_dora_report.pdf (p. 40); summary at https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report · Oct 22, 2024 · independent-ish research programme run by Google Cloud; survey of ~39,000 professionals; Bayesian estimates, correlational · high · Ch 1
2. **DORA 2024: process measures improved at the same time.** "A 25% increase in AI adoption is associated with … 7.5% increase in documentation quality, 3.4% increase in code quality, 3.1% increase in code review speed, 1.3% increase in approval speed, 1.8% decrease in code complexity." DORA called this surprising, because historically these measures "lead to improvements in software delivery." · same PDF, pp. 38–40 · Oct 2024 · DORA survey · high · Ch 1
3. **DORA 2024's batch-size hypothesis.** "since AI allows respondents to produce a much greater amount of code in the same amount of time, it is possible, even likely, that changelists are growing in size. DORA has consistently shown that larger changes are slower and more prone to creating instability." · 2024 PDF p. 40 · Oct 2024 · DORA (hypothesis, not measured) · high (that DORA said it); medium (as mechanism) · Ch 1, Ch 10
4. **DORA 2024: individual gains are real but small, and valuable-work time falls.** "Productivity … is likely to increase by approximately 2.1% when an individual's AI adoption is increased by 25%". DORA also reports that time spent on valuable work decreases while toil stays unchanged (its "vacuum hypothesis"). · 2024 PDF pp. 32–33 · Oct 2024 · DORA survey · high · Ch 1, Ch 4
5. **DORA 2024 trust.** "39% of the respondents reported little to no trust in AI-generated code." Over 75% rely on AI for at least one daily task. · https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/devops-sre/announcing-the-2024-dora-report · Oct 22, 2024 · DORA survey · high · Ch 1
6. **DORA 2025: throughput turned positive and instability still rises.** "AI adoption now improves software delivery throughput, a key shift from last year. However, it still increases delivery instability. This suggests that while teams are adapting for speed, their underlying systems have not yet evolved to safely manage AI-accelerated development." · https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/2025_state_of_ai_assisted_software_development.pdf (p. 4, v2025.2); announcement https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report · Sep 23, 2025 · DORA; ~5,000 respondents surveyed Jun 13–Jul 21, 2025, plus 100+ h qualitative · high · Ch 1
7. **DORA 2025 on where friction moves (key quote for Ch 1).** "For an individual, friction doesn't vanish so much as move: It shifts from manual grind to deciding and verifying, possibly in the form of prompt iteration, result vetting, and assessing code that looks remarkably similar to correct code." 10. **DORA 2025: local gains are lost downstream.** "Without this foundation, AI creates localized pockets of productivity that are often lost to downstream chaos." · 2025 PDF p. 3 · Sep 2025 · DORA · high · Ch 1

## Cases

- **Google (internal).** Did: ML code completion, ML-resolved review comments, internal AI tooling. Measured: acceptance 37%, ~50% of code characters AI-completed, >8% of review comments AI-addressed (Google Research, Jun 2024), plus an RCT of ~21% faster task time (Paradis et al., Oct 2024). Measured by: Google itself. Result: about 25% → "well over 30%" of checked-in code involves accepted AI suggestions (CEO, Apr 2025). Illustrates: Coding L1–L2; Review L1–L2 (ML-suggested fixes, human applies). The research blog's framing that the author "becomes a reviewer" is direct evidence of the shift.
- **Anthropic (internal).** Did: org-wide Claude Code adoption. Measured: +67% merged PRs per engineer per day; self-reported +50% productivity; tool-call chain length up 116%, human turns down 33%. By: Anthropic (self-study). Result: more autonomy per task, but most staff can fully delegate only 0–20% of work. Illustrates: Coding L2 moving to L3 for routine work, with review still L2.
- **Faros cohort (multi-company).** Did: compared low- vs high-adoption periods within teams. Measured: telemetry across task trackers, VCS, CI/CD, incidents. By: Faros (vendor). Result: individual throughput up, review time and incidents up, no company-level gain (2025); review time ×5 and deploys −11.7% (2026). Illustrates: Coding L2 with Review/Testing at L0–L1, the failure mode Ch 1 describes.
- **METR OSS developers.** Did: RCT, then a rerun. Measured: task completion time. By: METR (independent). Result: −19% (early 2025); rerun inconclusive. Illustrates: Coding L1–L2 on mature, high-context codebases, where verification costs dominate.
- **Microsoft / Accenture / Fortune 100 field RCTs.** Did: randomised access to code completion. Measured: completed tasks (PRs). By: academic economists with Microsoft co-authors. Result: +26% tasks; juniors gain more. Illustrates: Coding L1.

## Contradictions & debates

- **Throughput sign flip, DORA 2024 → 2025.** It was −1.5% per 25% adoption in 2024 and positive in 2025. DORA attributes the change to people, teams and tools adapting. The populations differ (~39k vs ~5k respondents) and both years are cross-sectional surveys, so this is not a within-org trend. Instability is the one stable negative result.
- **Faros vs DORA.** Faros says its 2026 data "directly contradicts DORA's 2025 findings", meaning DORA's claim that strong foundations protect against AI's downsides. Faros is vendor telemetry; DORA is a survey. Neither is causal.
- **Review time: up or down?** Faros (+91%, then +441.5% median in review) and LinearB (agentic pickup 17.6 h vs 3.4 h) say up. Jellyfish says review time *fell* 5.1 h on average, and LinearB says AI-assisted (not agentic) PRs clear review *faster* (3.2 vs 4.2 h at P75). One possible reconciliation: wait and pickup time grows while active review time does not, and agentic PRs suffer most. Another possibility is shallower review. Faros notes its data cannot tell deferred review from shallow review, and reports that "PRs merged with no review" rose 31.3%.
- **RCT effect sizes span −19% to +56%.** The gains come from greenfield or standardised tasks with less-experienced developers (Peng; Cui). The losses come from experienced developers on their own mature repos (METR 2025). The Google RCT (+21%) sits in between. The book should present task type × familiarity as the moderator, not quote one number.
- **METR's 2026 rerun is not a "reversal".** Secondary coverage reports a speedup. METR itself says the signal is unreliable because developers refused AI-free work. That refusal is itself evidence of perceived value, and of how hard RCTs have become.
- **Quality: GitHub RCT (small gains) vs GitClear, CodeRabbit and Uplevel (degradation).** Every one is a vendor with a commercial stake. GitHub's is the only randomised study, but it used a short, bounded task. The degradation studies are observational and cannot attribute changes to AI.
- **"% of code written by AI" is not productivity.** Google says 25–30%+ of code involves accepted AI suggestions, yet its "engineering velocity" figure is about 10% (*secondary*, see leads). The gap between the two numbers illustrates the chapter's point.

## Open questions for the book

1. Which DORA metric should Ch 17 recommend as the "AI tax" signal: change failure rate, rework rate (new in 2024–25), or time-in-review? DORA now publishes rework rate distributions (2025 PDF p. 21).
2. Can the book cite one *independent, telemetry-based* study of review queue growth? Every review-time number found here is from a vendor (Faros, LinearB, Jellyfish). DORA's evidence is survey-based plus a hypothesis.
3. How to present "% of code AI-generated" (Google 25–30%+, Anthropic, Microsoft) without implying it equals productivity. Proposal: one sidebar on "volume metrics vs flow metrics".
4. Should Ch 1 frame the shift as *verification* (DORA's "deciding and verifying") or *review* (Faros, LinearB)? The evidence supports verification broadly, covering review, testing and the integration of larger batches.
