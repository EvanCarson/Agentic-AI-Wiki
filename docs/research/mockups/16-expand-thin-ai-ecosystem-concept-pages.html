Concepts · The AI Model & Tooling Ecosystem

Serving & access: APIs, local inference, and gateways.

This entry separates two things people routinely conflate: which model you use and where it runs. You will leave with a clear map of the access layer — first-party APIs, cloud-hosted model catalogs, independent inference providers, local/self-hosted serving, and gateways — and be able to read a three-axis trade-off grid (control, cost, latency) to select the right serving path for your workload.

TL;DR: pick the model on capability; pick the serving path on data boundary, cost at your volume, and concentration-risk tolerance. These are two separate decisions. Treat them that way in your architecture from day one.

STEP 1

Model choice and serving choice are orthogonal.

A common mistake is treating "use Llama" or "use Claude" as a single decision. In reality you pick a model and, separately, a way to run it. A given open-weight model might be available from several independent inference companies, from cloud catalogs, and on your own hardware — same weights, very different latency, price, privacy, and reliability. Decoupling these two decisions is the core skill of this entry.

The practical consequence: your model evaluation and your serving evaluation should use different criteria, run at different cadences, and involve different stakeholders. Conflating them leads to lock-in that is expensive to undo and to serving choices made on capability grounds rather than operational ones.

STEP 2

The five access patterns.

First-party provider API

Call the lab that trained the model directly. You get the newest snapshots first, the provider's full feature set (tool use, caching, reasoning controls), and their reliability engineering. Trade-off: only their models, their pricing, their availability and roadmap. The default for closed frontier models.

Cloud-hosted model catalogs

Major cloud platforms expose many models (often including third-party and open-weight ones) behind a unified, in-cloud endpoint. Value: data stays in your existing cloud boundary, unified billing and IAM, enterprise compliance. Trade-off: model availability lags the first-party source by a cycle or more.

Independent inference providers

Companies specializing in serving open-weight models fast and cheap, competing on latency, throughput, and price per token. Value: often the cheapest/fastest route to a popular open model, no infrastructure to run. Trade-off: you are trusting a third party with your data and uptime.

Local / self-hosted serving

Run open-weight models on your own hardware. Value: maximum control and privacy, fixed cost at high utilization. Trade-off: you own capacity planning, scaling, batching efficiency, security patching, and uptime — a real engineering function, not a checkbox.

Gateways / routers

A proxy layer that presents a single API and adds cross-provider concerns: key management, fallback and load-balancing, spend caps, caching, and unified logging. Value: provider independence from one place. Trade-off: another component in the hot path to operate and secure.

STEP 3

The dimensions that decide it.

  • Data boundary. Where is inference data legally and contractually allowed to go? Often the first filter, forcing self-hosting or in-cloud serving regardless of cost.
  • Cost model. Per-token opex (APIs, inference providers) vs fixed capacity (self-host). Crossover depends entirely on sustained utilization.
  • Latency and throughput. Independent providers compete hard here; first-party varies; self-host is whatever you engineer it to be.
  • Feature lag. First-party gets new model snapshots and capabilities first; intermediaries lag by a cycle or more.
  • Reliability and concentration risk. A single provider is a single point of failure. A gateway with fallbacks trades simplicity for resilience.
STEP 4

The three-axis trade-off: direct API vs gateway vs self-host.

Most teams will eventually face the question of whether to add a gateway layer or move part of their traffic to self-hosted open weights. The table below reads across three axes — control, cost, latency — for the three main serving postures. Use it to identify which axis is your binding constraint before choosing a path.

Control & privacy Cost model Latency profile
First-party / inference provider API Low — data leaves your perimeter; provider terms govern retention and use Variable — per-token opex; cheap at low volume, expensive at sustained high throughput Good — optimized serving infra; predictable p50; p99 varies under shared capacity pressure
Gateway / router in front of multiple providers Medium — adds key rotation, audit logging, policy enforcement; data still exits your perimeter Variable + overhead — same per-token cost plus gateway opex; breaks even if fallback avoidance reduces failed calls Good + small penalty — adds one hop (~5–20 ms); buys fallback routing on provider outage
Self-hosted open-weight model High — inference stays in your infra; full control over data handling, patching, and access Fixed capex — GPU capacity is a fixed cost; cost per token drops sharply at high utilization; break-even typically at ~50% sustained GPU util Variable — depends entirely on your hardware and batching efficiency; can outperform cloud APIs at scale, or underperform if under-provisioned 
# Break-even rough estimate: self-host vs. API
# Assumes H100 at ~$3/hr, 40k tokens/min throughput
api_cost_per_1m   = 0.80   # $ per 1M output tokens (typical mid-tier model)
gpu_cost_per_hr   = 3.00   # $ per GPU-hour (spot/reserved)
tokens_per_hr     = 40_000 * 60  # tokens/min → tokens/hr

api_cost_per_hr   = (tokens_per_hr / 1_000_000) * api_cost_per_1m
# break-even: api_cost_per_hr >= gpu_cost_per_hr
# i.e. you need > 3.75M tokens/hr (~62k tok/min) to make self-host cheaper

The break-even point for self-hosting moves dramatically with GPU spot pricing, model size, and batching efficiency. Run this calculation for your actual token volume before committing to GPU capacity. Teams that self-host prematurely tend to underestimate the engineering cost of running inference at a reliable SLA.

STEP 5

The pragmatic starting posture.

For most teams starting out: call the first-party API of whichever model passes your evaluation, behind a thin internal abstraction of your own. That abstraction is the cheap insurance — it lets you later move a model behind a gateway, add a fallback provider, or self-host the high-volume path without rewriting your application. Premature multi-provider routing is over-engineering; an un-abstracted hard dependency on one endpoint is under-engineering. The thin seam between them is the right starting posture.

Decide the model on capability and your eval. Decide the serving path on data boundary, cost at your volume, and reliability needs. Keeping these two decisions separate — and seam-able in code — is what keeps the access layer from becoming lock-in.
