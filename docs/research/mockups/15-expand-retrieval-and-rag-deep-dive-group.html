New page sketch — hybrid-search-bm25-vs-vectors
Deep Dive · Retrieval & RAG

Hybrid Search: BM25 vs Dense Vectors

Most production retrieval problems require both exact-term matching and semantic similarity — not one or the other. This essay explains why pure sparse (BM25) and pure dense (vector) retrieval each fail in predictable ways, how reciprocal rank fusion merges their ranked lists into a single hybrid ranking, and how to tune the fusion alpha when your workload skews toward one retrieval regime.

PART 1

Why dense retrieval fails for lexical queries — and why BM25 fails for semantic ones.

Dense retrieval encodes queries and documents into a shared embedding space and retrieves by cosine similarity. It handles paraphrase and semantic equivalence gracefully: "heart attack" and "myocardial infarction" land near each other. But it systematically underperforms on queries where exact token presence is the signal. Ask a dense retriever for "RFC 2616" or "error code 0x80004005" and it will return documents about HTTP or Windows errors in general — semantically adjacent, lexically wrong.

BM25, the canonical sparse model, operates on token frequency and inverse document frequency. It excels at the lexical query: product codes, error identifiers, named entities, version strings. Its failure mode is the converse — it cannot bridge semantic gaps. "How do LLMs hallucinate?" retrieves documents about hallucination directly; "Why do language models fabricate facts?" may score poorly if "hallucinate" does not appear in the corpus.

Neither retriever dominates the other in the general case. Enterprise corpora in particular tend to contain both types of query: semantic questions from analysts and exact-identifier lookups from engineers. The practical conclusion is that you need both running in parallel and a strategy for merging their outputs.

PART 2

Reciprocal rank fusion: merging two ranked lists.

Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) is the standard merging strategy. For each document, its RRF score is the sum of 1 / (k + rank) across all retrievers that returned it, where k is a small constant (typically 60) that dampens the influence of the top-ranked documents and prevents any single retriever from dominating. Documents retrieved by both systems get a score from both terms; documents retrieved by only one get a single term. The final list is sorted by descending RRF score.

# Reciprocal Rank Fusion (k=60 is the conventional default)
def rrf_merge(bm25_results, dense_results, k=60):
    scores = {}
    for rank, doc_id in enumerate(bm25_results, start=1):
        scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    for rank, doc_id in enumerate(dense_results, start=1):
        scores[doc_id] = scores.get(doc_id, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(scores, key=scores.get, reverse=True)

RRF requires no score normalization between retrievers — ranks are ordinal, not cardinal — which makes it robust across retriever implementations that produce incomparable raw scores. Its main limitation is that it treats both retrievers as equally reliable; for workloads with a strong prior toward one regime, a weighted variant is appropriate.

RRF with k=60 is a sensible starting point for almost any hybrid setup. Tune k down (toward 10–20) only if your top-1 precision matters more than recall depth, and prefer the weighted variant over raw RRF only when you have offline evaluation data confirming the skew.

PART 3

When BM25 wins and when dense wins — and tuning the alpha.

Weighted hybrid retrieval replaces RRF with a linear combination of normalized scores: alpha * dense_score + (1 - alpha) * bm25_score, where alpha in [0,1] controls the blend. Alpha = 1 is pure dense; alpha = 0 is pure BM25; alpha around 0.5–0.7 suits most mixed workloads.

Signals that should push alpha toward BM25 (lower alpha): your queries frequently contain product identifiers, error codes, version strings, or other low-frequency exact tokens; your corpus is a structured knowledge base with controlled vocabulary; your users are developers or analysts running precision-oriented lookups.

Signals that should push alpha toward dense (higher alpha): your queries are natural-language questions; your corpus is unstructured prose where synonym variation is high; you have invested in domain-adapted embeddings that narrow the semantic gap for your topic area.

The reliable way to set alpha is offline evaluation against a labeled query set. Without one, start at alpha = 0.6 and measure top-5 recall on a representative sample of production queries before committing to a value.
