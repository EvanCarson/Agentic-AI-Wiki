Part IV · The Career

Where FDEs Work

Two offers with the same FDE title can be different jobs: one turns your field work into product, the other bills your hours. The employer type changes the job more than the title does, so place the employer first: it decides what the work is for, how the pay is built and where you can go next. Then compare salaries.

15.1

Where the openings are

Start with a correction. The FDE job market is not mainly the labs and Palantir. It is mostly companies you have not heard of, selling one product into enterprises that cannot install it alone.

The best census of postings comes from Plank, which on 12 September 2026 counted 1,333 live FDE posts at 565 companies and linked each one. About two-thirds were in the United States, and 9% sat at the frontier labs and Palantir combined (Plank, FDE job market, September 2026; T2, a measured count published by a firm that sells FDE services). Bloomberry, working from about 1,000 posts in an analysis published ten months earlier, found 58% of FDE roles at companies with 11 to 200 employees (Bloomberry, November 2025, updated January 2026; T2). The two samples cover different windows and use different rules, so do not combine them. They agree on the shape: the typical FDE opening is at a growth-stage company, not a lab.

Counting by title distorts the picture in both directions, and a count made for this book shows how.

FDE-titled roles, read from employers' own job-board feeds on 17 October 2026 (T1 as a count of what was advertised; it says nothing about hires). Roles with "forward deployed" in the title, out of all open roles: Palantir 77 of 310; OpenAI 26 of 814; Mistral 15 of 217 (16 earlier the same day; the counts move by the hour); Scale AI 14 of 182; Cohere 11 of 118; Anthropic 6 of 655; ElevenLabs 4 of 156; Harvey 3 of 345; Glean 3 of 134; Sierra 0 of 186; Decagon 0 of 143. The OpenAI Deployment Company's own board listed 6 FDE-titled roles out of 19.

Undercounting. Anthropic listed 50 roles with "Applied AI" in the title, against its 6 FDE-titled ones. Sierra advertises the work as "Strategist, Agent Development" and Decagon as "Agent Deployment Engineer".

Overcounting. Of Palantir's 77, 32 are internships, new-grad posts or "Year at Palantir" placements, and 28 were created more than two years before the count. The oldest, a London Forward Deployed Software Engineer post, was created in December 2009. Plank's own caveat says the same: one posting can stand for many seats or for an evergreen pipeline.

An echo. A September 2026 川观新闻 summary of a 霞光智库 industry report gives "565 companies" hiring FDEs and a US median base of $193,000 (川观新闻, September 2026; T3). Those are Plank's census figures, relayed without attribution. Count them once.

Growth figures are left out on purpose. They measure different windows, several echo one Indeed series, and Appendix D lists them unblended. For choosing an employer they don't help anyway: a market can double while the job you are offered is still the wrong one.

15.2

Four employer types, by what they buy with your time

Origins and Economics sorted employers into three economic models. This chapter asks what each does to your week, your pay and your next job. It also adds a fourth type the economics chapter left out, the application startup, because 15.1 shows it is where most openings are.

The test: what is the employer buying when it pays for an hour of your time?

  • An AI lab (OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral, Cohere) is buying two things: production use at a few accounts large enough to matter, and evidence about where its models fail. The customer already pays for the model. Your hours are an investment in usage and in signal.
  • An application startup (Sierra, Decagon, Harvey, Glean and hundreds of smaller firms) is buying deals and a working deployment of its own product. Your hours are a cost of sale today and, if the product absorbs what you build, a product investment tomorrow.
  • A Palantir-style platform is buying account expansion. Pilots run at a loss by design, and your hours are justified when an account grows into a platform licence that runs without you.
  • A deployment company or FDE consultancy (the labs' 2026 spin-outs, and the consultancies that have adopted the title) is selling your hours. Delivered outcomes are the product, and utilisation is the cost line.

Real companies mix these. Place an employer by where it leans, and check the lean with the sheet in 15.8. The types predict three things a title doesn't: who sets your priorities, what earns you a raise, and what you will be good at when you leave.

15.3

What the posts say the work is

Job posts are T4 evidence of how the work is done and primary evidence of what employers ask for. Read across the four types, the differences show up in what each post says success is.

Labs measure production and signal. OpenAI's San Francisco FDE post says: "You will measure success through production adoption, measurable workflow impact, and eval-driven feedback that changes product and model roadmaps" (OpenAI, FDE - SF, September 2026). Anthropic's Staff FDE post says "Customers bring you in to lead, not only to implement: you decide what should be built, and then make sure it gets built well" (Anthropic, Staff FDE, October 2026). The lab job is the closest to this book's definition: few accounts, deep ownership, and a duty to report back. Its risk is the one Origins and Economics names for labs: serving one large customer so well that nothing comes back to the product.

Application startups measure the deal. Decagon's Engineering Lead, Strategic Deployments will "own the technical build for 1–3 Strategic Deals end-to-end, operating like the founding CTO of each", find technical risks "and de-risk them before they threaten the deal", and turn "bespoke deal requirements into durable improvements to the core Decagon product" (Decagon, July 2026). Sierra's Flagship Deployment strategist is told to "drive revenue upside for Sierra, and work internally to manage down cost to serve over time, improving margins" (Sierra, September 2026). Glean's founding FDE post makes the product promise outright: "The best of what you build ships to every Glean customer in the world" (Glean, first published January 2026). The startup FDE sits closer to revenue than the lab FDE, and closer to the product: there is one product, and your customer's gap is its roadmap. The risk is that the deal wins every argument with it.

Platforms measure expansion, and hire young. Palantir's board is the only one in the sample with a full pipeline: internships, new-grad posts, a year-long placement, and specialised forward deployed roles in infrastructure, reliability, security and customer enablement, with Deployment Strategist posts, the non-coding partner role, listed alongside. Its FDSE post still describes responsibilities "similar to those of a startup CTO" (Palantir, FDSE - US Government). The platform job teaches the most and specialises earliest. Its risk: if the platform never absorbs the field work, the FDE is a consultant with stock.

Deployment companies measure delivery, and say what they fear. The OpenAI Deployment Company calls itself "the deployment arm of OpenAI", operating "at the intersection of cutting-edge AI, enterprise transformation, and scaled services delivery". Its founding FDE will "own both technical and business value outcomes end-to-end" and "ramp quickly toward management responsibilities". It wants candidates who "can make disciplined standardization-versus-customization decisions instead of defaulting to bespoke work" (OpenAI Deployment Company, FDE - US, June 2026). That is the most candid line in the sample: a company whose revenue is engineering capacity knows bespoke work is its natural drift, and is hiring people to resist it. A second post invites "an established team of two to five people" to apply together, and warns that "People who apply together may work on different deployments" (DeployCo, Apply with Your Team). That is how a services firm staffs: it hires capacity and allocates it.

Where you sit during the week also differs by type, and here the evidence conflicts.

Time on site. OpenAI tells new FDEs to expect "around 50% of their time on site"; some in the UAE and Japan are on site "around 80% of the time", and people in Europe or the US "might spend 2-3 weeks per month with customers" (Gregor Ojstersek, August 2026; T3, relaying Colin Jarvis). The Pragmatic Engineer reports that Palantir "expects around 25% of FDEs' time to be spent onsite with customers" and Commure "up to 50%" (Pragmatic Engineer, August 2025; T3). Nabeel Qureshi, who joined Palantir as an FDE in 2015 and left in 2023, says FDEs were "typically expected to 'go onsite' to the customer's offices and work from there 3-4 days per week" (Qureshi, October 2024; T4). The Palantir figures disagree, and may describe different eras.

Posted. OpenAI FDE: travel "up to 50%". Anthropic FDE: 25–50%. Palantir FDSE: 25–50% "preferred, but requirements vary". Glean: hybrid, "3-4 days a week" in an office, plus 25–50% travel. Decagon: "We're an in-office company". DeployCo FDE: three office days a week and up to 50% travel. Harvey: "2–3+ times per month… typically… 1–3 days at a time".

The usable pattern, an inference from a handful of posts and one relayed interview: labs and deployment companies send you to the customer; startups keep you near the product team and send you out in bursts. If you can't travel half the time, that rules out more lab and deployment-company jobs than startup ones. Ask for the last quarter's actual travel, not the posted ceiling.

15.4

What pays: read the mix, not the midpoint

Posted base pay overlaps heavily across the four types. The differences that matter are in how the package is built and in what the width of the band hides.

Posted bands, US base unless marked, read 17 October 2026. Labs: OpenAI FDE (including healthcare, legal and financial-services variants) $185K–$300K plus equity; OpenAI FDSE, New York and Seattle, $153K–$325K; Anthropic Staff FDE $280K–$320K; Anthropic FDE London £225K–£255K; Scale AI's three-level ladder $180K–$225K, $216K–$270K and $252K–$315K; Cohere FDE CA$175K–CA$385K. Application startups: Decagon Agent Deployment Engineer $175K–$230K (London £110K–£150K); Decagon Engineering Lead, Strategic Deployments $200K–$400K; Sierra Strategist, Agent Development $200K–$290K, with the post not saying whether that is base or on-target; Glean Founding FDE $160K–$270K, where "Certain roles may be eligible for variable compensation, equity"; Harvey Forward Deployed Finance Engineer "OTE $254,600 - $350,000 USD (75/25 Split)", with nothing on what the variable part is paid on. Platform: Palantir FDSE and Forward Deployed AI Engineer, estimated $135K–$200K, which "may also include Restricted Stock units, sign-on bonus"; new-grad FDSE $135K–$145K. Deployment company: DeployCo FDE $170K–$400K plus equity; London £90K–£150K; Paris €115K–€200K.

Aggregates. Plank's median posted base midpoint is $193K, from the 388 of 1,333 posts that disclose pay (29%). By funding stage it runs from about $150K at seed to about $243K at frontier labs, with Series B at $235K. Plank stresses these are base only and that equity is the larger part of a lab package; that share is Plank's estimate, not a posted figure (T2). Bloomberry's median, from its earlier sample, is $173,816 (T2). Different samples and windows: do not average them. Bloomberry also reports that 8% of posts mention OTE and "exactly 0%" are quota-carrying, while calling its 30% "Sales Engineer+" type "quota-carrying". Its own figures disagree; treat the variable-pay share as unknown.

Four rules turn bands like these into something you can use.

A wide band means the level is decided in the offer. A rule of thumb: when the top of a band is more than about 1.5 times the bottom, the employer is hiring across several levels with one post. Glean's post says so outright: "This range encompasses varying levels of the role, you will be assessed for your level during the interview process". Your number depends on the level they place you at. Ask which level the offer is at, what the next one requires, and who decides. The rubric in The Skill Map gives you the evidence to argue a level, not a number.

Variable pay tells you whose targets you share. On-target earnings mean part of your pay rides on something commercial. That can be fine for a domain-expert FDE who carries an account from pilot to daily use. First ask whether the posted range is base or on-target earnings; some posts don't say, and Anthropic's carry a standard line that "For sales roles, the range provided is the role's On Target Earnings" without saying whether the FDE role is one. Then ask what the variable part is paid on: bookings, renewals, usage or delivery. If it is bookings, the job is pre-sales, and Chapter 1's checklist will probably say the same.

Equity is a bet on the product loop. Startup stock pays only if the product scales past the custom work, which is what the field-to-product loop exists to produce. Before you value it, ask for one feature that started in the field. If no one can name one, the stock is a bet against your job's purpose.

Lower base can be the platform's design. Qureshi's account of below-market Palantir salaries recovered in stock is set out in Origins and Economics (T4, one alumnus, with the stock's later rise as a confound). The platform model asks you to wait for the account and the company to mature together; whether that pays depends on the company.

15.5

Upside and exits

First, the gap. Nobody has followed a cohort of FDEs to see where they went next: no attrition data, no tenure data, no study of exits by employer type. What follows is anecdote, employer claims and this book's inference, each labelled.

What the record shows. At the platform that invented the role, the best-documented exit was into the product. The Pragmatic Engineer reports that "Up until circa 2016, Palantir had more FDEs than it had 'normal' software engineers", and that after Foundry launched, "more FDEs went back to working as software engineers, working on Foundry and bringing their experience from the field" (T3). Qureshi argues that "former FDEs tend to be great founders", and adds that there are "usually more ex-Palantir founders than there are ex-Googlers in each YC batch" (T4; he gives no count, and an alumnus is not a neutral witness). Employers now recruit from that exit in reverse: Anthropic's, Glean's and Decagon's posts all invite former founders to apply, and DeployCo invites whole teams. Anthropic's Staff FDE post describes a senior individual path that leads "engagements delivered with partners" (T4 throughout).

The inference. Your exit follows what you owned, and the employer type decides what you can own. At a lab you own production at a few demanding customers and the evidence that changed a product or a model. That leads to product and research-adjacent roles, or to founding a company in a vertical you now know from the inside. At a startup you own deals and a product's gaps in one market, which leads to early-employee and founding roles in that market, or to running deployment at the next startup. At a platform you learn the most kinds of customer earliest, and the exit is into the platform's product or into founding. At a deployment company you own delivery across many customers, which leads to engagement management, delivery leadership and the consulting partner track. Leaving it for product work is harder unless your field work demonstrably fed one.

That last point is where the sceptics are right. The disappointed hires in Gergely Orosz's reporting, one of whom quit after four weeks, describe a job with "a typical IT services mindset" (Pragmatic Engineer, March 2026; T4, two anecdotes). What an FDE Is argues that such a job has dropped the stages that make it an FDE job. For a career the cost is concrete: two years of services work under an FDE title prepares you for more services work. That can be a good career, but not the one most applicants think they are choosing. Levels, Burnout and What Comes Next takes the ladders and the exits further.

15.6

China: one title, a fourfold-to-tenfold spread

The Chinese market makes this chapter's argument more starkly than the US one. What an FDE Is showed the same title filed under product, R&D and sales support. Origins and Economics showed a market led by in-house teams at the large platforms.

The titles already sort employers by type. Tencent posts 前线部署工程师; ByteDance posts "AI FDE工程师" in R&D, and one of its posts is titled "豆包AI大模型FDE", tied to its own model. Those are the in-house platform and lab end of the market. At the other end, an industrial software company in Tangshan advertises an "人工智能交付工程师（FDE）": an AI delivery engineer, which is the services end named in the title itself.

Posted monthly pay, H1 2026. 中新经纬, republished by 36Kr in June 2026, read postings on 猎聘 and BOSS直聘: ByteDance's 豆包AI大模型FDE at ¥35,000–70,000 a month over 15 months (up to ¥1.05 million a year); Ant Digital's B-end FDE at ¥40,000–60,000 a month over 15 months; Zhipu's FDE lead at ¥60,000–80,000 a month (中新经纬 via 36Kr, June 2026; T3, a journalist reading posts). In the same article a practitioner at a Shanghai AI company, under a pseudonym, puts junior FDEs at ¥20,000–30,000 a month and senior FDEs mostly on annual packages above ¥400,000; interviewees say million-yuan packages are concentrated among top talent and are "not the industry average" (T4).

Single source. The xdash open book adds Tencent Cloud at ¥35,000–65,000 and Alibaba Cloud at ¥20,000–50,000 over 16 months, and the Tangshan post at ¥8,000–16,000 a month, requiring a master's degree and on-site work at manufacturers (xdash, chapter 1, August 2026; T3). Its ByteDance, Ant and Zhipu figures are identical to 中新经纬's, so they are an echo, not confirmation. Its source appendix has no links, so the Tencent Cloud, Alibaba Cloud and Tangshan figures could not be checked.

Growth. 川观新闻 cites 脉脉 data that FDE-related postings rose 21-fold year on year in H1 2026 (T3, low). It is a different platform, market and window from every US figure. Don't set it beside them.

Two lessons carry over. First, annualise: Chinese offers quote monthly pay and a number of months, 15 or 16 at the large firms, so a lower monthly figure can be the larger offer. Second, the spread within one title is wide. On the checkable figures it is about fourfold, from ¥20,000 a month for a junior FDE to ¥80,000 for a model company's FDE lead; with the unchecked Tangshan post it is close to tenfold, the figure xdash itself draws. Either way the spread is the employer type showing through more than seniority does. As xdash puts it, ask whether a company "按结果收钱，还是按人头收钱": does it charge for results, or for heads? That is this chapter's question in one line.

15.7

The comparison grid

Each row is a lean, not a verdict.

Employer type
The job, and what pays
Upside, exits, and the trap
AI lab
The job: production at a few large accounts, plus evaluated evidence that changes the product or the model. Heavy travel. Pays: high base, with equity the larger part by Plank's estimate; variable pay rarely stated, so ask.
Upside: the hardest problems and the most visible signal. Exits: product, research-adjacent roles, founding in a vertical. Trap: one customer served so well that nothing comes back, and code the next model release makes obsolete.
Application startup
The job: win and land deals for one product; the customer's gap is the roadmap. Close to the product team. Pays: base near lab levels by Series B, equity that depends on the product scaling, sometimes on-target earnings.
Upside: the most direct line from field work to product. Exits: early employee or founder in the same market; head of deployment elsewhere. Trap: the deal wins every argument with the roadmap, and you become pre-sales.
Palantir-style platform
The job: expand accounts until they run on the platform; specialise early; work beside a non-coding strategist. Hires from university. Pays: lower base, stock, and a long wait for both to mature.
Upside: the broadest training, the most kinds of customer earliest. Exits: the platform's product team, or founding. Trap: the platform never absorbs the field work, and you are a consultant with stock.
Deployment company or FDE consultancy
The job: deliver outcomes across many customers; hired as capacity and allocated; quick path to managing others. Pays: the widest bands, set by level; in some markets a lower floor than labs.
Upside: volume. Many engagements fast, and early management. Exits: delivery leadership, engagement management, partner track. Trap: bespoke work as the default, utilisation as the measure, and a title that no longer separates you from IT services.

Read the grid against your skill self-assessment. If your weakest domain is product, a deployment company will keep it weak unless its field work demonstrably reaches a product. If your weakest is customer, a startup forces it up fastest. Choose the type whose trap you are least exposed to and whose exit you want.

Most answers are ASK. For a company months old that is normal, not a reason to walk, but get them from your future lead before you sign: the post alone can't separate a deployment company that feeds a product from one that sells capacity. And the one fully answered line, the band, is the least informative.

The insurer's FDE, a year on. The FDE from Part II has two offers: a founding role at a deployment company and a deployment role at an application startup in insurance claims. On posted base the two sit within a few tens of thousands of each other. The FDE fills in two sheets. The deployment company's trap check comes out at three of five, with no named field-to-product change. The startup's comes out at one, because its lead can name the claims-intake feature that began as a customer workaround. The FDE's self-assessment from month nine showed product as the weakest domain, a drift toward services. The FDE takes the startup offer at the lower base, for the domain it will force up, and asks for the next level's criteria in writing. The patterns behind this beat are the shape check in The Skill Map and the deployment-company post's own warning against "defaulting to bespoke work".

A reader who wants to manage early could reasonably take the other offer. The sheet doesn't choose; it makes the choice about the job rather than the title. If you are still in the loop, Getting Hired covers what it tests.

Before you compare salaries, write down what the employer is buying with your hours. Production and signal, a deal, an expanding account, or your capacity: each one leads to a different week, a different pay mix and a different next job. Pick the type whose trap you can resist and whose exit you want, then negotiate the level, because inside a wide band the level is the pay.
