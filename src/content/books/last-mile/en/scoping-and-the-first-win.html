Part II · The Engagement

Scoping and the First Win

A first win nobody agreed to measure cannot be won, so fix the metric, its baseline and its threshold before writing any agent code. Then pick a target the business would miss if it failed, cut it down until it fits in weeks, and write down everything you cut. The scope is a one-page contract with three names on it, and this chapter ends with a template for it.

6.1

What this phase is for, and how it fails

Discovery, the previous chapter, ends with an observed problem: the thing that actually costs the customer time or money, which is rarely the thing they asked for. Scoping turns that problem into something you can win. That means a workflow small enough to build in weeks, a number that says whether you built it, and the signatures of the people whose judgment of "better" counts.

Scoping fails in two directions, and each looks like a reasonable choice when you make it.

The safe target. The team picks a workflow nobody minds failing: an internal FAQ, a side process, a department with no budget. It ships, and nobody notices. Colin Jarvis, OpenAI's head of forward deployed engineering, describes the opposite pattern in the customers who succeed: "they don't do like a kind of edge case like over to the side of the company. They pick something with genuinely high stakes" (Altimeter interview, November 2025; T2, a vendor describing its own customers). His example is Morgan Stanley, whose wealth-management practice is one of the bank's largest businesses. A win on a side workflow proves only that the model works on a side workflow, and nobody has to believe it.

The unagreed number. The team picks a real target but never fixes what success means. The demo goes well, everyone says it looks promising, and then the debate over whether it is good enough runs for months. RAND's practitioner interviews, which Discovery covered, put this at the root of the most common failure. Stakeholders "misunderstand—or miscommunicate—what problem needs to be solved using AI", and models end up "optimized for the wrong metrics" (RAND, 2024; T1, though it rests on interviews, not on project outcomes). In scoping the error looks harmless: a model tuned to a number nobody agreed, which becomes obvious only after it is delivered.

Those two failures pull against each other. A target with high stakes tends to be large, and a large target does not fit in a pilot. Scoping is how you hold both: keep the stakes and shrink the surface. Pick the workflow the sponsor cares about, then cut it to one slice of it, with one number and one date.

6.2

The composite: one line of business, one number

Midway through week two at the insurer. Discovery found that adjusters spend somewhere between a third and a half of each day triaging incoming claim documents, and that the chatbot the sales team promised was a proxy for "claims take too long". The COO wants all claims faster. The FDE wants something that can be measured within the 30-day paid pilot the FDE and the lead negotiated. They meet in the middle: first-notice-of-loss document triage for auto claims only. Auto claims arrive in the highest volume, from a few hundred to a few thousand documents a week, and their documents (police reports, photos, repair estimates, the claim form) are more uniform than property claims. Property goes on a written "not now" list, with the reason next to it. The pattern behind this beat is Jarvis's "genuinely high stakes": claims handling is the insurer's core operation, not a side process.

The number. The success metric has two parts: routing accuracy (did the claim reach the right queue?) and time-to-first-action (how long from the document's arrival until an adjuster first acts on it?). Neither figure is in any report, but both can be measured fast. Time-to-first-action comes from the same claims-system timestamps the FDE pulled in week 1 for the COO's cycle-time baseline, and on a sample of a few hundred recent auto claims it runs from one to three days. Routing accuracy has no record at all, so two or three senior adjusters label the same sample independently in the days after the scope is signed. Claims cycle time stays the COO's KPI, but a 30-day pilot cannot move it measurably; time-to-first-action is the slice of it the pilot can move, so the threshold sits there, and the week-4 readout reports both against the week-1 baseline. The claims director, not the COO, sets the threshold, because the director's adjusters will live with the routing. The director will not accept "as good as possible". She agrees to "at least as accurate as my senior adjusters are today, no litigation-flagged claim ever routed to the fast queue, and first action on the same working day instead of one to three days later". The pattern is the success-criterion shape in Anthropic's Claude documentation: a metric, a threshold, a held-out set, and an improvement over baseline.

The demo. Read access to a replica of the claims system has not arrived; the next chapter is about why. The day-5 data term has slipped, and the lead invokes it: the 30-day clock pauses for the replica, and the COO agrees to proceed meanwhile on a redacted export, so the readout moves with the pause. The FDE does not wait. Claims operations exports a small batch of real auto claims with claimant details redacted, and at the end of week 2, once the scope is signed, the FDE demos the routing on those documents to the claims director and the senior adjusters, opening each claim in a mock of the adjuster's queue. The pattern is Alpine Edge's four-week plan, which puts one complete workflow in week 2.

Three things in that beat carry the chapter. The target sits in the core business but is cut to one line of it. The number is two-dimensional and measured before anyone builds. The person who sets the threshold is the person whose team pays for a wrong answer. The rest of this chapter shows how to get there.

6.3

High stakes, small scope

The best evidence that small starts work comes with a catch. Stanford's Digital Economy Lab studied 51 enterprise AI deployments that delivered measurable value, and found that "73% of implementations started small deliberately, and 63% framed their pilots explicitly as experiments" (Pereira, Graylin and Brynjolfsson, April 2026; T2, and the copy online is marked as a draft). The catch is in the title: Lessons from 51 Successful Deployments. The sample contains only successes, so it cannot tell you how many failed projects also started small. Read it as "successful teams usually started small", not "starting small makes you succeed".

The authors' explanation is more useful than the percentages. They call controlled scope a "failure strategy": "Small pilots fail cheaply. Cheap failures do not end careers." In one of their cases, a professional-services firm with two failed technology projects behind it moved forward with a sponsor who "accepted 80% accuracy as good enough to move forward". A small scope makes an honest threshold politically possible.

MIT's NANDA report points the same way from the vendor side. Its takeaway is that startups that succeed "land small, visible wins in narrow workflows, then expand" (MIT NANDA, July 2025; T2). The method limits how far that goes. The report rests on interviews with 52 organisations, surveys of 153 leaders and a review of 300+ public initiatives, and it calls its own figures "directionally accurate", based on interviews rather than official company reporting.

None of this argues for a safe scope. Small and visible is not the same as small and peripheral. Jarvis makes the point again with OpenAI's European semiconductor engagement: "you got to pick a big a big kind of problem to solve" (Altimeter interview, T2). The way to hold both is to cut along the business, not away from it:

  • One line of business out of several (auto, not property).
  • One step of a long workflow (triage, not settlement).
  • One region, one team, or one share of traffic, with the rest kept on the old process.
  • Propose, don't act: the agent suggests and a person decides, which shrinks the risk without shrinking the stakes.

There is a test for whether you cut too far. Ask the sponsor what they would do if the pilot hit its threshold. If the answer is "roll it out to the rest of the claims operation", the scope is a slice of something that matters. If the answer is a shrug, you have picked the side workflow.

One more piece of evidence sets the frame. RAND advises leaders to "commit each product team to solving a specific problem for at least a year. If an AI project is not worthy of such a long-term commitment, it most likely is not worth committing to at all" (RAND, T1). A 30-day pilot does not contradict that. The first win is the opening of a year-long commitment to a problem, not the whole of it. If nobody at the customer is prepared to stay on this problem for a year, a successful pilot will have nowhere to go.

6.4

The metric before the build

Write the success criterion before the first line of agent code. Once a prototype exists, every conversation about the threshold turns into a conversation about the prototype, and the number drifts toward whatever it already does.

Anthropic's guide to defining success criteria gives a shape worth copying exactly (Claude docs; T2, vendor guidance). Its example of a well-defined criterion reads: "The sentiment analysis model should achieve an F1 score of at least 0.85 … on a held-out test set of 10,000 diverse Twitter posts … which is a 5% improvement over the current baseline." That one sentence holds four parts, and a scope needs all four:

  1. A metric that the business recognises, not just the model team: routing accuracy, minutes saved, cases closed.
  2. A threshold, written as a number or a rule with no room for interpretation.
  3. A held-out set that the threshold is measured on, which the builders never tune against.
  4. A baseline that the improvement is measured from (section 6.5).

The same guide adds that "most use cases need multidimensional evaluation along several success criteria." For an FDE this matters more than for a product team, because a single accuracy number hides the errors the customer fears most. Pair the main metric with a speed or cost metric, and with at least one guardrail: a class of error that must never happen, such as the composite's litigation-flagged claim reaching the fast queue. A guardrail is usually what the customer's risk function will ask about first, and setting it now saves an argument later.

Who sets the threshold. Not the FDE, and often not the sponsor. The threshold belongs to the person whose team absorbs the errors: the claims director, the head of the contact centre, the clinical lead. The sponsor signs that the metric matters; the operational owner signs that the threshold is good enough to use. If those are the same person, good. If nobody will own the threshold, you have found the workflow with no owner, and that is a reason to change the target, not to proceed.

Who owns the held-out set. The customer, always. Their experts label it, they keep it, and the vendor sees it only when the threshold is measured. That sounds bureaucratic, but it protects you as well as them. When the vendor builds both the agent and the test, a pass is a vendor claim. When the customer owns the test, a pass is the customer's own finding, and it is much harder to argue with at the conversion meeting. Seed it from the baseline sample in week 1, so that the same documents measure the old process and the new one.

Translate the threshold into the sponsor's words. "F1 of 0.85" means nothing to a COO. "At least as accurate as your senior adjusters, and same-day first action" does. Write both versions in the scope: the precise one for the eval, the plain one for the sponsor. If they ever disagree, the plain one is what you will be held to.

This chapter sets the threshold; the evals chapter (Chapter 9) builds the harness that measures it and grows the held-out set into an eval suite. The wiki's Evals 101 covers the vocabulary, and eval-driven agent development covers how an eval becomes the spec an agent is built against.

6.5

Measure the baseline in week 1

No baseline means no win. Without a measured "before", a good result can always be called what the old process would have done anyway, and a bad one can be blamed on a bad week. Alpine Edge's four-week template for a forward deployed engagement puts the baseline first: "Measure the baseline before any AI touches it: cycle time, rework, cost per task" (Alpine Edge, August 2026; T4, one consultancy's practice). Its four weeks are worth knowing as a shape:

  • Week 1: "Discover and baseline".
  • Week 2: "Build one complete workflow".
  • Week 3: "Harden and pilot".
  • Week 4: "Assess production readiness and results".

The same post lists the warning sign for this phase: "A prototype exists but no production-like data access by end of week one." Treat that as an alarm, not a delay. The data-access chapter that follows (Chapter 7) covers what to do about it; for scoping, the point is that a baseline measured on data you cannot reach is not a baseline.

A baseline does not need a data platform. It needs a sample, a stopwatch and a labeller:

  1. Sample. Pull a few hundred recent cases from the target workflow, spread across weeks and teams so that one bad Monday does not set the number. Write down how you drew them.
  2. Time. For speed metrics, take timestamps the systems already hold (received, assigned, first action, closed). Where none exist, shadow the people doing the work for a day and time it, and say in the scope that you did.
  3. Label. For accuracy metrics, have two of the customer's experts label the same cases independently. Where they disagree tells you two things: which cases are genuinely ambiguous, and the ceiling. No agent will be judged better than two experts agree with each other.
  4. Keep the sample. These labelled cases become the first version of the held-out set.

The baseline is also your first discovery check. If the measured number is far better or worse than everyone said in discovery, find out why before you set the threshold. Either the process is not what you were told, or the metric is not measuring what you think it is. Both are cheaper to learn in week 1 than in week 4. The SDLC book's chapter on the 90-day pilot makes the same argument for engineering teams: decide the comparison before the run, not after.

6.6

The "not now" list

Scope grows in every meeting. A stakeholder who was not in discovery asks whether it could also handle property claims. The sponsor's peer wants the same thing for underwriting. Someone points out that a chatbot was in the original proposal. Each request is reasonable, and each one, accepted, moves the first win further away.

Refusing does not work, because a refusal looks like a vendor protecting its margin. Writing it down does. Alpine Edge's mitigation for scope creep is one line: "One workflow until the core metric moves; keep an explicit 'not now' list" (T4). The list does three jobs:

  • It tells the person asking that the request was heard and has a place to go.
  • It turns "no" into "after the threshold is met", which gives the stakeholder a reason to want the first win.
  • It becomes the expansion plan. When the pilot succeeds, the "not now" list is the first draft of what comes next.

Write each item with a reason and a trigger: "Property claims: documents too varied for a 30-day pilot; revisit once auto meets threshold." An item with no trigger is a promise you have not decided to keep.

The list is not frozen. Jarvis describes OpenAI's approach to ambiguous engagements as "bias for moving fast, proving out any brick walls and then adjusting the scope to the most useful thing we can do" (Pragmatic Engineer, August 2025; T3, a secondary quote). Scope changes when you hit a brick wall, such as data that does not exist or a system that cannot be written to. It does not change because a new stakeholder came to the meeting. When it does change, change the one-page scope, the threshold and the signatures together, and record what moved onto or off the list and why.

Employers describe this trade-off as the core of the job. Their postings are primary evidence of what they ask for, though not of how the work is done (box below).

How FDE postings describe the scope trade-off. OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineer posting (Zurich, July 2026) asks the FDE to "Make trade-offs between scope, speed, and quality; adjust plans to protect delivery" (posting). Glean's Founding Forward Deployed Engineer posting (first published January 2026) asks for judgment on "when to simplify scope to get something real into production faster" (posting). Decagon's Engineering Lead, Strategic Deployments (July 2026) must "quickly assess how long different technical paths will take, weigh their risks, and choose between them under pressure", across work that runs "from POC through close and into post-close stabilization" (posting). All three are T4 for how the job is done; postings change or close, so check them before you quote them.

6.7

The first demo

The first demo is a communication tool before it is a technical milestone. Its job is to make the scope real to the people who will use the result, early enough that they can still change it. Four rules make it work.

Show the real workflow on real or redacted data, never a slide. A deck asks the audience to imagine the product; a demo on their own documents lets them judge it. Synthetic data is fine for building, but a demo on synthetic data alone tells the customer's experts nothing they can trust, because they cannot see their own edge cases in it. If access is still pending, ask the data owner for a small redacted export. It is a much smaller request than a replica, and it is usually granted faster.

Demo what the user does next, not what the model can do. The adjuster does not care that the model extracted every field on the form. They care that the claim arrived in their queue, already sorted, with the police report on top. Open the demo in the screen they work in, or the closest mock you can build, and walk through their next action.

Say out loud what isn't done. Name the "not now" items, the cases the prototype gets wrong, and the fact that nothing has been measured against the threshold yet. Alpine Edge lists a second warning sign, which it calls the proof-of-concept trap: "Demo excitement, no baseline" (T4). A demo that runs ahead of the measurement creates enthusiasm the numbers then have to live up to. Saying what is missing keeps the threshold, not the applause, as the bar.

Pick the audience for the decision you need. The first demo goes to the threshold owner and two or three of the people who will use it, not to the executive committee. You want them to correct you. The executive demo comes later, with a number attached.

"The exec wants a demo on Friday" is a common pressure in week 1. Agree to it, but on your terms: real documents, the user's next step, and a slide-free statement of what is and is not measured yet. If you cannot show anything real by Friday, show the baseline instead. A measured "before" is a stronger thing to put in front of an executive than an unmeasured "after".

After the demo, the first exposure to real use should also be small. WeightWatchers, working with Sierra, planned to test its customer-service agent on "a small percentage of traffic", "a hundred or so inbound inquiries at a time" and "a small set of use cases" before deciding whether to widen it (Sierra customer story; T2, vendor-published, and the page does not say who measured its results). The team then "quickly expanded the breadth of use cases". The page reports that the agent contained "nearly 70% of all cases within the first week". Treat that as the vendor's account of its own best customer; the useful part is the shape, which is a small, bounded slice of real traffic first and expansion only after it holds.

Each of these shows up early, usually in the first two weeks, and each points to one fix.

The threshold is "as good as possible"

Find the person whose team absorbs the errors and ask them to name the number, in their words.
No baseline by week 2

6.10

Ready to move on

Move into data access and the build when every box is ticked:

  • The metric, its baseline and its threshold are written down and signed by the sponsor and the threshold owner.
  • The held-out set exists, was labelled by the customer's experts, and has a named owner on the customer side.
  • The "not now" list has been agreed, with a reason and a trigger for each item.
  • The first demo is scheduled with real users, on real or redacted data.
  • A week-by-week plan exists, and each top risk has an earliest signal and a date.

Put the threshold in the sponsor's words, and get it in writing before the first line of agent code. Once a prototype exists, the threshold starts drifting toward whatever the prototype already does. A number the sponsor has said in their own words and signed is the only one that still means something in week 4.
