Part IV · The Career

Levels, Burnout and What Comes Next

Climbing an FDE ladder by volume, one more engagement, site and pager, repeats the same evidence and piles up the load that burns people out. The one employer whose posts distinguish levels asks its senior FDEs for leverage instead: work that happens without you. Nobody has published attrition data, so plan level, load and exits as one decision.

18.1

What the ladders say, and what nobody has measured

Start with what is missing, which is most of the subject. No employer, analyst or researcher has published how long FDEs stay, how many leave, why, or where they go; this book's research looked for primary attrition and tenure data and found none. Burnout evidence is anecdote, and the nearest survey covers a neighbouring role. Job posts are primary evidence of what employers ask for and T4 evidence of how the work is done; this chapter uses them mostly for the first.

The published ladders disagree about what a level is.

A ladder by years. Scale publishes three FDE levels as three separate posts. Apart from the title, the minimum years and the pay band, the three are the same text, word for word, "Technical Leadership" section included. On paper a staff engineer does a senior's work with more years behind them, and each band "may be inclusive of several career levels". That shows how one employer writes posts, not how it promotes, but it tells a candidate nothing about what changes between levels.

A ladder by behaviour. Anthropic's FDE and Staff FDE posts differ where Scale's do not. The FDE builds: "Work within customer systems to build production applications". The Staff FDE is told that "Customers bring you in to lead, not only to implement", and is asked to "Review designs and unblock other FDEs on hard technical calls" and to "Lead engagements delivered with partners, setting the architecture and the quality bar for work you don't build yourself". The requirements change from years (4+ for the FDE) to track records. The travel ask drops, from 25–50% to 25%. These are hiring asks, not promotion criteria; no employer publishes those.

No ladder in the title. OpenAI's individual FDE posts carry no level at all; only the two manager posts break the pattern. A single US band covers the title, nearly as wide as the span Scale splits into three. Palantir took this furthest. Nabeel Qureshi, who joined Palantir as an FDE in 2015 and left in 2023, describes a company that chose not to give people titles, and that as a result people "came in and out of fashion very quickly, inside the company" (Reflections on Palantir, October 2024; T4, one former employee's recollection).

The top of every ladder is narrow. In Bloomberry's count of posts that state experience, 60% ask for three to five years and only 8% for nine or more (T2; the full split is in Getting Hired). Most of the market hires the middle, which is where the volume trap in the next section catches people.

Ladders and bands, from the employers' own feeds (fetched 2026-10-17). Scale's "Frontier Agents Engineer (Forward Deployed Engineering)" ladder: 4+ years, $180,000–$225,000; Senior, 5+ years, $216,000–$270,000; Staff, 8+ years, $252,000–$315,000; base pay in San Francisco, New York and Seattle (FDE, Senior, Staff; first published May 2026, updated October 2026). The three texts differ only in title, years and band.

Anthropic: Forward Deployed Engineer, London, £225,000–£255,000, travel 25–50% (September 2026); Staff Forward Deployed Engineer, US, $280,000–$320,000, travel 25% (October 2026); Manager, Forward Deployed Engineering, New York, $320,000–$405,000, travel 25–50% (first posted February 2026, updated August 2026).

OpenAI: 26 posts with "Forward Deployed" in the title on its board, 24 of them individual roles with no seniority level in the title, plus two manager posts (New York; Tokyo, travel "up to 40%, although mainly local"). The US FDE band is $185,000–$300,000, including the San Francisco post (September 2026); the Forward Deployed Software Engineer posts run $153,000–$325,000 (New York, Seattle) and $185,000–$325,000 (San Francisco), and the Washington, DC government post $145,800–$280,000. The Manager, Forward Deployed Engineering post in New York pays $302,000–$385,000 with travel "up to 25%" (September 2026).

All figures are posted base ranges, before equity. They show what employers advertise, not what anyone is paid. Market-wide pay is in Where FDEs Work.

18.2

Leverage, not endurance

Put the Anthropic contrast next to the rubric from The Skill Map and any ladder becomes legible. Read that grid by who does the work:

  • L2: you do it, in their world. Real data, a real sponsor, a real engagement.
  • L3: you do it against someone with power. A security reviewer, a director, a sponsor who could have said no and didn't.
  • L4: it happens without you. Another FDE starts from your template, the roadmap carries your pattern, the company chooses its next customer partly on your case.

That is a ladder of leverage: the higher levels are the same work done through other people, other engagements and the product, not done harder. Anthropic's Staff post says so directly: "work you don't build yourself". The manager posts in Leading FDEs push one step further.

The trap is to climb by volume instead. Volume feels like progress: a third engagement, a fourth, more customer sites, more systems you know better than anyone. But each repeat produces evidence at the level you already hold. The Skill Map warned that "a great deal of experience that feels like FDE work is L1 done many times"; the mid-career version is L2 done many times. Five engagements in which you personally built the integration, ran the eval and briefed the director are five pieces of L2 and L3 evidence. None of them is L4, because in none of them did anything happen without you.

Where a ladder is written by years, as Scale's is, or not written at all, ask your lead three questions before the next review:

  • "What did the last person promoted to staff stop doing themselves?" An answer that names only tenure or customer count means the ladder rewards volume.
  • "Who has started from something I built, and would they say so?" No name means no L4 evidence, however many engagements you have run.
  • "Does anyone here get time between engagements, and what is it spent on?" This one also answers the load question in 18.4.

Vague answers to all three are information about the employer, not about you. Getting Hired has the versions to ask before you sign.

18.3

What the burnout evidence actually says

Burnout is the risk everybody names and nobody has measured. Here is all the evidence, graded.

One practitioner, relayed by a reporter. LeadDev's July 2026 piece on the role reports Piotr Kraus, an ex-Palantir FDE now at an FDE advisory firm, as saying the constant travel makes the role "susceptible to burnout a lot earlier than others"; the piece notes that he often worked in remote, hard-to-reach places. A recruitment-firm founder adds: "It's definitely not for everybody… It's a very challenging role" (LeadDev, July 2026; T3, and the burnout line is the reporter's paraphrase, not a direct quote). The article gives no attrition data. Its other much-quoted line, Chris Slovak of Unframe saying the model "doesn't seem very sustainable", is about the business model, scarce engineers placed in every customer, not about the people. It is not evidence of burnout.

One insider, with a counter-signal. Qureshi describes Palantir FDEs expected to "go onsite" to the customer "3-4 days per week", and says the field engineers "tend to have high pain tolerance" (T4). He also says the company "was loud about the fact that people worked long hours, were paid lower than market, and had to travel a lot", yet "it was common to stick around for 5+ years". That runs against the assumption that a hard field job must have high turnover, with a confound he names himself, the stock (Where FDEs Work reads it as a pay design). Long stays under heavy load may say more about equity than about stamina.

One adjacent survey. Consensus, which sells demo software, surveyed more than 600 presales and sales professionals and reports that "Burnout is a real and growing problem for SEs". It finds burnout higher in teams with six or more account executives, where sales engineers "struggle to keep up with growing demo demands", and at large enterprises (Consensus, September 2025; T2, vendor-reported, with no prevalence figure and no published method). Sales engineers are not FDEs. What carries across is the shape: burnout rose with the demand on each person and with repetitive work, which the report also names, not with the role as such.

One resignation that was not burnout. The disappointed hire in Gergely Orosz's reporting, who "quit 4 weeks later", left because the job was "a typical IT services mindset", not because it was exhausting (Pragmatic Engineer, March 2026; T4). Lumping that mismatch (What an FDE Is) in with burnout hides both.

That is all of it. Course sites make confident claims about weekly hours and cite nothing. The honest summary: the load is real and advertised, the damage plausible and unmeasured, and the only survey, of a neighbouring role, points at the load on each person.

18.4

The three loads, and how to cap each

What wears FDEs down comes in three loads. Employers advertise the first, barely mention the second and never mention the third.

Travel: advertised as a ceiling. Most lab posts state a travel percentage, and most of them say "up to". Bloomberry finds 68% of FDE posts require travel (T2), but puts its "builder" type at 30–50% in one place and FDEs at 50–75% in its own table, so quote neither as settled. A ceiling is not an average, and travel in an engagement is lumpy. Discovery wants you on the floor for days at a stretch (Discovery); the build can mostly run remotely; the handoff drill needs you in the room again (Production and Handoff). The book's rule of thumb, untested: cap travel by phase, not by percentage. Agree in the engagement brief which weeks are on site, and treat on-site weeks outside discovery and handoff as a signal that something else has gone wrong.

On-call tails: barely advertised. Of OpenAI's 26 forward-deployed posts on 17 October 2026, one, the financial-services post, mentions "on-call readiness". The others are silent, while the same board's infrastructure posts spell out the on-call rotation. Silence is not absence: the FDE's on-call is not a rotation anyone schedules, so nobody writes it into a post. Yet every system you build and do not hand off cleanly stays yours. After three engagements you can be answering for three systems while building a fourth. The cap is the dated support window from Production and Handoff: a scope, a channel and an end date, written down. A handoff without an end date is not a handoff. It is the second load waiting to start.

Consecutive engagements: never advertised. No post says how many engagements run back to back, or how many at once. The only public number is one team lead's remark of two to three projects per FDE (T4), quoted in Leading FDEs, which also proposes counting heavy phases rather than accounts. If the Consensus shape holds, the demand an FDE controls least is engagements with no gap between them. The cap is a gap, planned and named; the composite below asks for two to three weeks, a working figure, not a measured one. The best use of the gap is the work that produces leverage. Writing the product-feedback brief (Feeding the Product), turning a component into a template, reviewing another FDE's design: these are recovery from the field and L3–L4 evidence at the same time. An FDE who goes straight from one engagement to the next loses both.

That is why level and load belong in one plan. Climbing by volume and burning out are one mistake seen from two sides: the move that raises your level, doing more of the work through others, is the move that lowers your load.

Ask for all three caps in writing. If the answer to each is no, believe it. An employer that will not date a support window or leave a gap between engagements is probably selling your hours; the five questions in Origins and Economics will confirm it. If so, sustainability and promotion are both an argument with the business model, and the honest options are a different team or the exits below.

Travel and on-call, as posted (fetched 2026-10-17). OpenAI's FDE posts: "Travel up to 50% is required" on most, with the Tokyo post saying travel is "mainly within Japan"; the Financial Services post asks the FDE to own "on-call readiness across financial workflows". Anthropic: FDE "Travel frequently (25-50%)"; Staff FDE "(25%)". Palantir's Forward Deployed AI Engineer: "traveling up to 25%… but flexible based on personal preferences", base $135,000–$200,000; its New Grad US Government FDSE: "25-50% preferred, but requirements vary by team and location", $135,000–$145,000. Harvey's Forward Deployed Finance Engineer posts express travel as frequency, not percentage: "2–3+ times per month… typically… 1–3 days at a time" (October 2026). Scale's three ladder posts carry no travel line.

18.5

What comes next: the exits, and what each needs

Nobody has published where FDEs go. Bloomberry's profile sample describes where they came from, not where they went, and no employer has released alumni data. The map below is built from employers' asks and one insider's account, not outcomes. The question for each exit is which domain it draws on: an exit stays open only while you hold evidence there.

Exit
What it draws on
How to keep it open
Staff or principal FDE
Technical L4 and judgment L3: patterns others reuse, calls others defer to. Anthropic's Staff post is the clearest description.
Review other FDEs' designs; get one of your components started from by someone else.
FDE manager
Everything at L3, plus other people's engagements succeeding. Both lab manager posts ask for prior people management.
Lead a partner-delivered engagement; own the hiring loop once. See Leading FDEs.
Product engineering or product management
Product L3: a field pattern adopted into the roadmap. In Qureshi's account, Palantir's product engineers "productize" what FDEs build (T4).
Write the product-feedback brief yourself, every engagement.
Founder
Customer and judgment at L4: holding a sponsor through failure, calling a stop. Qureshi argues former FDEs "tend to be great founders" (T4, his opinion).
Own one rescue-or-exit decision with a sponsor, not only the analysis behind it.
Deployment company or consultancy
Customer L3 on its own. This is the exit that happens to you if product stays at L1.
Choose it deliberately, or keep product evidence alive so it stays a choice.

Two of these deserve a caution. The founder exit has the most enthusiastic advocates and the least data. Where FDEs Work sets out the record: one alumnus's argument, and employers, Anthropic among them ("Former technical founders are also encouraged to apply"), recruiting founders in the other direction. Nothing shows how many FDEs found companies, or how those companies fare.

The consultancy exit deserves the other caution. It is a respectable job, but it is the one you reach without choosing, one well-run engagement at a time; The Skill Map's filled self-assessment ended exactly there. Keeping exits open mostly means not letting any domain sit at L1 for a year.

One more reason to keep them open: the title may not last. A practitioner in China, quoted under a pseudonym, compares it to the product manager of the mobile-internet boom: "它大概火爆了五到十年，然后慢慢降温，我认为FDE也会经历类似的过程" ("it was hot for roughly five to ten years, then slowly cooled; I think FDE will go through something similar") (中新经纬 via 36Kr, June 2026; T4, one person's forecast relayed by a news outlet). It is one guess, but a fair reason not to let the title be your only evidence.

China: the level shows up in the pay form. The same 中新经纬 report, republished by 36Kr on 5 June 2026, quotes the pseudonymous practitioner on his Shanghai AI company: lower-level FDEs earn "2万元至3万元不等" (¥20,000–30,000) a month, while higher-level FDEs are paid mainly as an annual package, "多在40万元以上" (mostly above ¥400,000) (T4, one practitioner). The move from a monthly wage to a package is the level change, so compare offers annualised. The posted listings in the same report, for ByteDance, Ant Digital and Zhipu, are in Where FDEs Work, with their echo in xdash's book flagged.

18.7

The composite: the insurer's FDE plans the next year

Month nine, after the wind-down. The mid-level FDE fills in the growth plan the same week as the self-assessment in The Skill Map, after the lead struck two scores and the shape check came back "services". The load picture is ordinary for the role, which is the point. The FDE was on site through most of discovery and scoping and for the handoff drill, and a day or two a week in between, mostly for the model-risk review rather than the build. The auto triage system is the insurer's, but defects still come to the vendor through a ticket channel until the support window ends, a few months after handoff, which will overlap the first weeks of whatever comes next. The lead's month-eight review pencilled in two to three weeks on the template first (Leading FDEs), but sales has proposed a next engagement whose start date would swallow them. Nothing about the year was extreme; it simply has no gap in it, and the work that would raise the FDE's weakest level, the product brief, is exactly the work a no-gap year squeezes out. The plan is for the next year at the vendor, with the exits kept open; Where FDEs Work picks the FDE up after that next engagement, about eighteen months after the pilot, still short on product evidence and weighing two senior offers. The patterns behind this beat are Anthropic's Staff post ("work you don't build yourself"), the Consensus finding that sales-engineer burnout rose with demand on each person, and Kraus's account of travel bringing burnout sooner.
