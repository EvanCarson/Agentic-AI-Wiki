Part II · The Engagement

Building in Their Environment

On someone else's infrastructure, their constraints bind and your preferences do not. A rule the customer calls "never" cannot rest on a model that is right most of the time, so enforce it in plain code outside the model. Then build the least clever system that clears the eval and that their operators can run without you, and write both choices down where their platform team and model risk will sign them.

10.1

What this phase is for, and how it fails

By now the engagement has a signed threshold, a data path, a model-risk decision and a climbing eval. It lacks a system that runs where the customer runs things. This phase builds it: on their hosting, behind their identity provider, calling the endpoint they approved, shipped through their change process, and still working after the vendor's people go home.

The FDE does not choose the ground. A product engineer builds on infrastructure their own company picked; an FDE builds on infrastructure somebody else picked years ago, for reasons unrelated to AI. Those constraints are not obstacles to route around. They are the specification.

The phase fails in three ways, and all three come from treating the customer's environment as a deployment detail.

Importing your own stack. The design assumes the services the vendor uses at home: a managed vector database, a hosted orchestration framework, outbound calls to a public endpoint. Then the customer's platform team says none of those exist inside their network, and the design starts over in month two.

Rules in the prompt. The customer has a rule that must never break, and the team writes it into the system prompt in capitals. It holds in the demo and on most of the eval. "Most" is the problem. The first time it breaks in production, the conversation is with the customer's risk function, not with the eval.

Cleverness nobody can operate. The team builds an agent loop with tools, memory and a planner because it is interesting, when a single model call with a fixed output would clear the threshold. It works while the FDE is there to read the traces, and nobody can maintain it the week the FDE leaves.

A word on evidence. This is one of the thinnest chapters in the book: no measured study says which architectures succeed inside customer environments. What exists is secondary reporting on FDE teams (T3), one vendor's engineering guidance and one former FDE's memoir (both T4), and job posts, which show what employers ask for but not how the work goes. The argument rests on those, and on wiki pages for the stack it does not re-teach.

10.2

The composite: their VPC, their rules, a boring pipeline

Months two and three at the insurer. The annual contract is signed, with go-live gated on the routing-accuracy threshold, and the eval is still climbing from the low 80s. Production has to live inside the insurer's private cloud. The platform team sends its terms in the first week. Workloads run on its container platform, inside its VPC, with no outbound internet. Model calls go only to the in-region endpoint model risk approved. Every service authenticates through the insurer's identity provider, and changes ship through a weekly change board. The FDE's first sketch had assumed a managed vector store and the vendor's orchestration framework. Neither is on the platform team's list, and neither turns out to be needed.

The rules. Two of the claims director's rules are not negotiable. A litigation-flagged claim is never routed to the fast queue, and certain claim types always go to a human queue, whatever the documents say. The FDE keeps both out of the prompt entirely and writes them as a few dozen lines of ordinary code that run before and after the model. A litigation flag set in the claims system overrides any suggestion, and a suggestion naming a queue outside the allowed list is discarded. Each rule has its own tests, run on every change, and they must pass every time rather than score well. For the awkward case the senior adjusters picked for the eval, an attorney's letter in an unflagged claim's email thread, the model may send the claim toward a human queue, but nothing it says can move a claim away from one.

The pipeline. What runs in production is deliberately dull. A claim packet arrives and its text is extracted. One model call returns a queue and a short reason in a fixed format, and the rules check the result. The suggestion lands in the side table the insurer's team owns, with inputs, output and every version logged. If the endpoint is down or the output does not parse, the claim goes to the default human queue, which is what happened to every claim before the engagement. There is no agent loop and no retrieval index. The weeks of eval work that follow change the text extraction, the prompt, the examples and the rules, never the shape of the pipeline. The production stack runs in shadow while the eval closes the gap, so go-live, when the threshold is met, is a configuration change. The patterns behind this beat are the deterministic hard constraints reported from an OpenAI FDE engagement with an APAC automotive manufacturer, and Anthropic's advice to find "the simplest solution possible".

Three choices carry that beat. The platform team's terms became the design brief. The rules left the model for code that can be tested to 100%, and the model kept only the authority to be more careful. The architecture stopped where the eval said it was enough.

10.3

Inventory their constraints before you design

The defining fact of the FDE's build work is where the code lives. Gergely Orosz reports that OpenAI's FDEs "write code directly on customer infrastructure and use customer tooling for this". Solutions architects, by contrast, "rarely write code on customers' infrastructure" (Pragmatic Engineer, August 2025; T3, secondary reporting from interviews). Anjor Kanekar, a Palantir FDE for seven years, "was on the final assembly line at aerospace manufacturer Airbus", and the piece notes "that many of his peer FDEs worked in similarly unconventional environments, including airgapped ones" (T3).

Employers write the same expectation into their postings. Sierra's Deployed Infrastructure Engineer needs a "track record of managing deployments in customer-owned cloud environments, including navigating security reviews, compliance requirements, and change management processes" (posting, April 2026; T4, evidence of what the employer asks for, not of how the work goes). The same post wants someone who thinks "about upgrades, rollbacks, monitoring, and failure modes from the start, not as an afterthought". At the far end of the range sits cleared, on-site and air-gapped work, where the constraints are absolute (box below).

What public-sector FDE postings require. OpenAI's Forward Deployed Software Engineer, Gov (posted September 2025) asks for "Active TS/SCI clearance or equivalent" and familiarity with "cloud deployment models (Azure, AWS), Kubernetes, Terraform" (posting). Anthropic's Applied AI Engineer, Public Sector will "Work primarily on-site with the customer's engineering and mission teams in secure government facilities", requires "Active TS/SCI clearance with Polygraph", and prefers "Experience deploying and operating software in air-gapped or classified environments" (posting). Cohere's Forward Deployed Engineer, Infrastructure Specialist (Public Sector), an Ottawa-based role posted September 2026, will "Lead end-to-end deployment of North in private cloud and on-premises environments", asks for production Kubernetes and Helm, and says "Active Top Secret clearance strongly preferred" (posting). All are T4 for how the job is done; postings change or close, so check them before you quote them.

Most engagements are not air-gapped, but the lesson transfers: learn what the environment forbids before you draw a box. Ask the platform team, in the first week of the build, for written answers to seven questions:

  1. Hosting. Where can a workload run: their cloud account, their container platform, their data centre, a vendor-hosted tenant? Who provisions it, and how long does that take?
  2. Model endpoint. Which endpoint did model risk approve, in which region, at which version? Who is told when it changes? If the approved option is a model they host themselves, the wiki's self-hosted inference page covers what that adds to the build.
  3. Identity. Which identity provider do services and people authenticate through, and who issues a service identity? A shared vendor key is the wrong answer.
  4. Network. Is there outbound internet access? Is there a proxy, an allow-list, a package mirror? Can your build pull dependencies at all?
  5. Data stores. Which stores and queues already exist that you are allowed to use? A new database is a new thing they must back up, patch and audit.
  6. Change process. How does a change reach production: a pipeline, a change board, a release window? How long from merge to running?
  7. Operations. Who will be paged when it breaks, and what tools do they already watch?

Write the answers into the context section of the decision record (10.7) before you write its decision. Every answer removes options, and that is the point: a design that respects all seven is usually smaller than the one you would have drawn alone, and it is the only one that survives the change board. Where the system must run untrusted inputs or code, the wiki's sandbox and isolation patterns cover the options; the customer's platform team decides which of them they will run.

10.4

Put the rules that must hold outside the model

Every engagement turns up a few rules that must never be broken. A litigation-flagged claim never goes to the fast queue. A payment above a limit always needs a second approver. A critical component always has at least two suppliers. The customer will say these rules out loud in discovery, and scoping often writes them down as guardrails. Before the build, get the full list from the threshold owner with one question: "Which wrong answer would you have to explain to a regulator, a court or the board?" Write each answer as one sentence with a trigger, an action and an owner. The build then decides where each one lives.

The best-documented field example is an OpenAI FDE engagement with an automotive manufacturer in Asia-Pacific. ZenML's LLMOps database summarises it from an interview with Colin Jarvis, who leads the team (ZenML, 2025; T3, a secondary summary of the vendor's own account, with no outcome metric reported). The team built a supply-chain agent on one principle: "use determinism wherever possible and LLMs only where their probabilistic nature adds value." Hard constraints such as "always maintaining at least two suppliers for critical components like tires" were "checked 100% of the time through deterministic code rather than trusting the LLM." The model did not optimise the plan directly either; it was "given access to a simulator" and explored options, while code enforced the limits. One case, secondhand, with no result attached: treat it as a pattern worth copying, not as proof that it works.

The reasoning does not need the case, though. A model's output is a distribution, and the best eval result is still a percentage. A rule the customer calls "never" cannot be satisfied by a percentage, so it has to sit in code that runs on every request and does the same thing every time. In practice that code goes in three places.

  • Before the model. Facts the system already knows decide the route without asking the model. If the claims system has set the litigation flag, the claim goes to the litigation queue, and the model's opinion is not requested or is ignored.
  • After the model. The output is parsed against a fixed schema, and only values from an allowed list survive. A queue name the rules forbid for this claim type is replaced by the human queue. A malformed answer is treated as no answer.
  • Around the action. The model's authority stops where the rule begins. In the composite the agent only proposes; an adjuster acts. The wiki's human-in-the-loop page covers where approvals sit, and policy-as-code for agents covers rule engines for when the rules outgrow a few dozen lines.

The hard case is a rule whose trigger only the model can see. A litigation flag is a field; an attorney's letter in an unflagged claim's email thread is not. Code cannot read the letter, but it can limit what the model's reading is allowed to do. Give the model authority in one direction only. It may move a case toward caution, such as sending a claim to a human because a document mentions counsel. It may never move a case away from caution: it cannot clear a flag or send a held claim down the fast path. A false alarm costs an adjuster a minute; a miss is what the rule exists to prevent. Write the permitted direction beside the rule in the decision record.

Test the rules separately from the eval. The eval reports routing accuracy as a percentage and is allowed to be below 100%. The rule tests are ordinary unit tests: one case per rule, plus the edge cases the customer's experts name, and the build fails if any of them fails. Reporting them inside the eval percentage hides the one failure that matters among the hundreds that do not.

This is also what keeps the security review's decision standing (see the security chapter). A rule in code is something a reviewer can read, test and sign. A rule in a prompt asks the reviewer to trust a distribution, and a careful reviewer will not.

10.5

Build the least clever thing that clears the eval

Once the rules are out, what is left for the model is usually smaller than expected: read a messy document, choose from a short list, give a reason. That job rarely needs an agent.

Anthropic's engineering guidance, drawn from work with dozens of teams building "LLM agents across industries", says so: "we recommend finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed" (Anthropic, December 2024; T4, a vendor's guidance from its own customer work, not a measured comparison). It suggests developers "start by using LLM APIs directly: many patterns can be implemented in a few lines of code." Frameworks, it warns, "often create extra layers of abstraction that can obscure the underlying prompts and responses, making them harder to debug." And it names the cost of autonomy: "The autonomous nature of agents means higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors."

An FDE has an extra reason to take that advice. Every layer you add is a layer the customer's team must understand after you leave, inside an environment where they cannot install your favourite debugging tool. Nabeel Qureshi's memoir of Palantir makes the point from the other side. His first real customer engagement was Airbus, where he "moved out to Toulouse for a year and worked in the factory alongside the manufacturing people four days a week". The software pulled "disparate sources of data — work orders, missing parts, quality issues" into one place. He is candid about how ordinary that was: "These are all sort of basic software things", yet "just deploying these ‘best practice’ UIs to the real world is insanely powerful" (Qureshi, October 2024; T4). His "4x" for the A350 manufacturing pace is a former employee's recollection with no method; do not repeat it as a result. The admission is the useful part: the value came from basic software fitted to the real workflow.

Use a ladder, and climb a rung only when the eval tells you to:

  1. Rules alone. If code can decide a share of the cases from facts already in the system, let it, and send only the rest to the model.
  2. One model call with a fixed output. A prompt, a few examples, a schema. Most classification, extraction and routing work stops here.
  3. A fixed chain. Several calls in a set order, each checkable on its own: extract, then classify, then check. Add a step when the eval's failure categories show one stage failing.
  4. An agent loop. The model chooses tools and steps. Reach for it only when the path through the task genuinely cannot be known in advance, and expect the eval, the audit log and the handoff to get harder.

The wiki's agents vs chatbots vs workflows page draws the same lines. The test for climbing is the failure categories from the evals chapter, not curiosity. If the routing errors cluster in documents the extraction mangles, fix the extraction; a planner will not help. If the customer already exposes the systems you need as tools, an MCP server can be the cleanest interface (what is MCP), but only on a rung where the model genuinely calls tools.

One caution. "Least clever" means least clever that clears the eval. A design too simple to reach the threshold is not modest; it is a failed engagement with tidy code. The ladder exists so that you climb when the numbers say so and not before.

10.6

Build it so they can run it

Qureshi is honest about the habit this phase must break: "FDEs tend to write code that gets the job done fast, which usually means – politely – technical debt and hacky workarounds" (T4). In a customer's environment, that debt becomes theirs the day you leave. Five habits keep it small.

Ship through their pipeline from the first week. If go-live is the first time the change board sees the system, you will spend the go-live window in their queue. Push something trivial through their full path early. Test in a staging environment inside their network that mirrors production; the wiki's staging environments for agents page covers what that environment needs.

Pin every version, and log it. The model endpoint, the prompt, the examples, the rules and the code each get a version, and every suggestion's audit entry records all of them. When the platform team upgrades the endpoint, rerun the eval and the rule tests before the change reaches production. A routing result you cannot reproduce cannot be defended to model risk.

Fail closed, to the old process. When anything goes wrong (the endpoint times out, the output fails the schema, a rule rejects it), the case goes where it went before the engagement. The customer's operations already know how to handle that path. Failing to the old process turns an outage into a slower day instead of an incident.

Use what they already run. Their logging, their monitoring, their secrets store, their queue. A component they do not already operate is one more thing to hand over, and one more reason for the platform team to say no.

Re-measure on the production stack. The eval number from the pilot was measured somewhere else. Have the threshold owner's team run the held-out set through the production deployment, inside their environment, and confirm it matches before anyone talks about go-live. Differences in text extraction, endpoint versions or timeouts show up here, not in the demo.

None of this is new to an infrastructure engineer. What is particular to the FDE is doing it on ground you do not control, for a team that will own it after you. The next chapter turns that ownership into an acceptance test.

One record per significant decision, written before the build commits to it and signed by the people who will live with it. The customer's platform owner signs that it fits their environment; model risk signs that the rules are where they approved them; the FDE lead signs that it can clear the eval. 10.9

Ready to move on

Move into production and handoff when every box is ticked:

  • The system runs inside the customer's environment, deployed through their pipeline under an identity their IT security issued.
  • Every rule that must hold is enforced in code outside the model, with tests that pass on every change; where only the model can see a rule's trigger, it can push cases toward caution and never away.
  • The architecture decision record is signed by the customer's platform owner, model risk and the FDE lead.
  • Every version is pinned and logged with each decision, and the fallback to the old process has been tested.
  • The held-out set, re-run on the production stack, matches the pilot result, and the threshold decides go-live.

If breaking a rule would end in a complaint, a fine or a courtroom, it does not belong in a prompt. Write it as code the reviewer can read, test it on every change, and let the model work only inside the space the rules leave open. The model can be wrong some of the time; the rules cannot be.
