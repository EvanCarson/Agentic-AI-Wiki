Agent inventory: the control that every other control assumes you already have.

Every governance mechanism you have built silently assumes a list of agents exists — a kill switch you cannot aim is not a kill switch, and a policy that runs on the agents you know about is a policy with an unbounded exception list. The mistake is treating inventory as documentation, which produces a spreadsheet that is stale before the survey closes. The fix is structural: make registration the way an agent obtains its credentials, so the inventory is a byproduct of deployment rather than a thing someone maintains, and reconcile it against traces so the gap between declared and observed becomes your finding rather than your blind spot.

STEP 1

Enumeration is a precondition, not paperwork.

Regulators arrived at the same requirement from their own direction. Under the EU AI Act, providers of high-risk systems listed in Annex III must register themselves and the system in the EU database before it is placed on the market or put into service (Article 49, using the structured information in Annex VIII, in the database established by Article 71); deployers that are public authorities, or act on their behalf, must additionally register their use of such a system. ISO/IEC 42001 approaches it as management-system hygiene: an AI management system starts with an inventory of every AI system you develop, deploy or consume — third-party models and AI features embedded in vendor tools included. That last clause is the one that quietly triples the list. (This is a map, not legal advice; see EU AI Act for agents and NIST AI RMF for agents.)

But compliance is the weakest reason to do this, and if it is your only reason you will build the wrong artifact. The engineering reason is that every operational control you already rely on takes a list of agents as an input, and degrades silently when the list is incomplete. Your kill switch halts the runs it can address. Policy enforcement applies to the call paths routed through the enforcement point. Incident response begins with "which agents touch this system," a question you answer in minutes or in days. None of these fail loudly when an agent is missing from the list — they just quietly do not cover it, and the coverage gap is invisible from inside each control.

STEP 2

The survey is stale before it closes.

The standard approach is a quarterly questionnaire to team leads, and it fails for a specific reason: agents are not created by deploys. A new agent appears when someone adds a tool to an existing config, points a client at a new MCP server, enables an integration in a SaaS product, or copies a prompt into a scheduled job. None of those look like "launching a system" to the person doing it, and most of them do not pass through the review that a new service would.

So the survey measures something real, but it is not what you asked. It measures which team leads replied, about the agents they personally remember, at one instant, using whatever definition of "agent" each respondent invented. By the time it is collated, the config has moved. The characteristic result is an inventory that is simultaneously incomplete (the shadow agents nobody reported) and inflated (rows for agents that were switched off two quarters ago and are still carrying live credentials).

The dangerous half of a stale inventory is not the missing rows — it is the rows that outlived their agent. An entry for a decommissioned agent whose token was never revoked reads as governed, is counted as covered in your control evidence, and is in practice an unowned credential with tool access. Retiring an agent is an inventory operation, and if it is not one, you accumulate authority with no owner.

STEP 3

Put registration on the credential path.

An inventory that people maintain out of diligence decays at the rate diligence decays. An inventory that people maintain because nothing runs without it does not decay at all. The design is one sentence: the registry issues the identity. No registration record, no client credential; no client credential, no gateway route, no tool scope, no model access. Registration stops being a form you fill out after shipping and becomes the first step of shipping.

This is not a new mechanism, which is why it is cheap. You are already being told to give each agent a distinct, scoped identity rather than a shared API key (agent identity and permissions, scoped credentials for agents). Issue those credentials from the registry and the inventory is a side effect of a thing engineers already want. The registry becomes the join key that makes the rest legible: the same identifier appears in traces, in the audit trail (audit trails and provenance), in the cost attribution, and in the kill-switch namespace.

# registry/agents/invoice-triage.yaml — the record that mints the credential
id: agent.finance.invoice-triage        # the join key: traces, ledger, kill switch
owner: "team-finance-ops"                  # a team, never a person who can leave
purpose: "Classify inbound invoices; route exceptions to a human."
autonomy: proposes                      # proposes | acts-with-approval | acts
model:
  platform: anthropic-api               # (platform, model_id) — see model-deprecation
  model_id: claude-opus-4-5-20251101
capabilities:                           # the authority, enumerated
  - tool: erp.invoices.read
    data: [vendor-name, amount, invoice-id]
  - tool: erp.invoices.flag              # write, reversible
personal_data: true                     # drives the DPIA / records question
review:
  human_gate: exceptions-over-10k
  eval_suite: evals/invoice-triage       # no suite ⇒ no production credential

Make two fields non-optional and refuse to mint a credential without them: an owning team and a named eval suite. The owner field is what prevents orphans, and requiring it to be a team rather than an individual is what keeps it valid after a reorg. Requiring the eval suite is how you stop the registry from becoming a list of agents nobody can measure — it puts eval-driven development on the critical path instead of the wish list.

STEP 4

Register the authority, not the agent.

Here is where most registries are built one level too coarse. "The agent" is a stable object: it has a name, an owner and a purpose, and those rarely change. But every question anyone actually asks the inventory is about authority, and authority changes constantly. The unit worth versioning is the (agent, capability, data-scope) triple.

The difference shows up the first time someone adds a write tool to an existing agent. At agent granularity that is an edit to a row — no new registration, no review, no diff. At triple granularity it is a new capability grant with a new risk posture, which is exactly what it is: an agent that could only read your ERP yesterday can now change it. The failure mode of coarse registration is that an agent's risk classification is set at birth and never revisited, while its actual authority accumulates for years.

Get the granularity right and the queries governance needs become one-liners against the registry rather than a research project:

  • Which agents can take an irreversible action without a human gate? The human-in-the-loop question, answered by data instead of by memory.
  • Which agents process personal data, and which of those cross a border? Feeds the data governance and residency work directly.
  • Which agents are pinned to a model with a retirement date inside 45 days? The same registry field that satisfies the auditor drives the CI check in model deprecation and migration.
  • Which agents have no owning team, or no eval suite, or have not run in 90 days? Three questions, one answer: revoke the credential.
STEP 5

Reconcile declared against observed, or you have a document.

A registry populated only by declaration is a record of intentions. The control emerges when you diff it against reality, and you already have the second source: traces (agent observability) stamp an agent ID, a model, and a tool on every step. Two directions, two different findings:

-- observed but not declared: shadow agents and un-reviewed capability creep
SELECT agent_id, tool, count(*), min(ts) AS first_seen
FROM spans WHERE ts > now() - '7 days'
  AND (agent_id, tool) NOT IN (SELECT agent_id, tool FROM registry_capabilities)
GROUP BY 1, 2 ORDER BY first_seen DESC;

-- declared but not observed: dead rows still holding live credentials
SELECT r.agent_id, r.owner, r.last_seen
FROM registry r WHERE r.credential_active
  AND (r.last_seen IS NULL OR r.last_seen < now() - '90 days');

The first query is your shadow-agent detector and it will embarrass you the first time you run it, usually with a capability that was added in a config change six months ago. The second is your revocation queue. Run both on a schedule, route the output to the owning team, and the inventory stops being a snapshot and becomes a converging process — the only kind that survives contact with a fast-moving codebase.

One caveat worth stating plainly: reconciliation only sees the call paths your telemetry covers. An agent that reaches a vendor API directly, outside your gateway, is invisible to both queries — which is the real argument for routing model and tool traffic through a chokepoint you own. The chokepoint is not there for the metrics; it is there so that "not in the traces" can be read as "not happening" instead of "not instrumented."

STEP 6

Build the small version, and put it in the path today.

This does not need a platform. It needs a directory of YAML files in version control, a CI job that refuses to mint a credential for a record missing an owner or an eval suite, two reconciliation queries on a weekly cron, and a rule that the gateway rejects an unregistered client ID. Version control gives you the change history and the review workflow for free — a capability grant becomes a pull request, which is precisely the review you were trying to create.

The failure mode to avoid is building the comprehensive schema first. A registry with six well-chosen fields that gates credential issuance is worth more than forty fields nobody fills in, because the six are true. Add a field when a question you actually had could not be answered — and delete any field you have never queried, because an unqueried field is not governance, it is a form.

Start from the credential, not the questionnaire. List every place an agent gets authority today — API keys, service accounts, OAuth clients, gateway routes, MCP server configs — and make each of them require a registry ID. That single change converts inventory from a recurring cost you pay in survey responses into a property of the system, and it is the difference between a list you defend to an auditor and a list you can actually operate a kill switch from.
