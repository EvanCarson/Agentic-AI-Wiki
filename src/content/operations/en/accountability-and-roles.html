Accountability and ownership — the agent is never the accountable party.

Every agent in your estate has a named accountable owner and not one of those names survives the follow-up question: accountable for what set of actions? The set is the tool catalog, the tool catalog changes on somebody else's release, and the sign-off does not. Name the owner, then pin what they signed for — a digest of the capability set at approval time — because an unpinned sign-off expires silently and nobody is notified.

STEP 1

Accountability does not transfer to the agent, and the platforms now enforce that.

An agent is an instrument; instruments are not accountable, the people who deploy them are. Saying "the AI decided" is, in governance terms, equivalent to "we deployed a system that took this action and we had not assigned anyone to own that". Autonomy does not create an accountability gap; it relocates accountability onto whoever chose to grant that autonomy, scope it, and ship it.

What changed in 2026 is that this stopped being a position you argue for in a document and became a required field in a directory. Microsoft Entra Agent ID will not create an agent identity without at least one sponsor — the business representative accountable for the agent's purpose and lifecycle — while the owner, the role that can change configuration and authentication, is optional. The accountable field is mandatory and the powerful field is not, which inverts thirty years of service-account practice. The sponsor's permitted actions are a short closed list: disable the identity, change its sponsors, soft-delete it. They cannot re-enable or restore, so "this agent was stopped" is a state only an owner or administrator can leave. Other platforms are converging on the same shape, and giving an agent a directory account is where most teams will meet it first.

Regulators arrived at the same primitive from the other direction. Article 26(2) of the EU AI Act requires deployers of high-risk systems to assign human oversight to natural persons with the necessary competence, training and authority, and to give them the necessary support. Read the three nouns as a specification: competence and training are hiring problems, and authority is the one that quietly fails, because authority over an unbounded action set is not authority. Those duties now apply to stand-alone Annex III systems from 2 December 2027 rather than August 2026 — see what moved in 2026 for the full date table and why the deferral is not a repeal.

STEP 2

The operator role: a named human owns this agent in production.

Every agent in production needs an operator — a specific, named human or role accountable for what it does while it is running. Not the team, not "AI governance" in the abstract: a person who can answer for this agent's actions, has the authority to pause or revoke it, and is expected to. The operator owns its scope and permissions, its escalation path, and the decision to keep it running after an incident.

Two properties make the role real rather than decorative, and both are cheap:

  • The stop is direct and the restart is not. Give the operator a one-click disable and withhold restore. A reviewer who must file a ticket to pause a misbehaving agent will instead watch it for another hour; an operator who can also un-stop it leaves a toggle rather than a record. The asymmetry is what makes "it was stopped at 14:12" evidence.
  • The field is validated at creation and re-validated on a cadence. A required owner stops an agent being born unaccountable; nothing stops it becoming unaccountable, because people change teams. Entra's own answer is a default inactivity policy that notifies at 60 days, disables at 90 and deletes at 455, plus an explicit "agents without owners" count in the admin centre — nobody builds a dedicated counter for a problem they think is rare. Your agent registry can enforce the same pair this week with a NOT NULL constraint and a weekly job listing rows where the human has left.

"Operator" is a governance role, not a job title — it can be an on-call rotation. The non-negotiable is that for any given action there is always a determinable human answer to "who was accountable for this".

STEP 3

RACI the autonomous action, not just the project.

Teams RACI the project and forget to RACI the action. For agent governance the unit that needs Responsible / Accountable / Consulted / Informed is the autonomous decision itself. For each class of agent action: who is Accountable (singular — the operator or their chain), who is Responsible for the agent behaving (the building team), who must be Consulted before high-consequence classes act (legal, security, a domain owner), and who is Informed when they do (audit, affected business owners).

# RACI per agent action class — Accountable is always singular
action_class        A              R            C              I
"read_only"           operator       eng-team     —              —
"refund <= 1k"       operator       eng-team     —              audit
"refund > 1k"        finance-lead   eng-team     fin-controls   audit
"delete_customer"    dpo            eng-team     legal,sec      audit

The table is correct and incomplete, and the missing column is the one the next step is about. "refund <= 1k" is not an action — it is a predicate over a set of tools that can move money, and that set is defined somewhere else, by someone else. Ask which tools satisfy it today and the honest answer in most deployments is "whatever the connected servers currently expose". Define action classes by scope and effect, then bind each class to an enumerated list of tool names, and the table starts describing something a person could actually accept.

STEP 4

The capability set is a moving target, so sign-off has to pin a version.

This is the load-bearing step. A sign-off is an assertion about a set of possible actions. That set is not in your repository.

Under MCP, a server advertises its tools through tools/list and may change them mid-session: the listChanged capability exists precisely so a server can tell clients to re-enumerate. The 2026-07-28 revision made the caching hints mandatory on list results — ttlMs and cacheScope — and the default is instructive: an absent ttlMs means the client treats the list as immediately stale and re-fetches. The protocol's own assumption is that your tool catalog is volatile. Vendors reword descriptions, add optional parameters and rename enum values with no commit in your repository and no error in your logs; tool drift is the operational treatment of that, and catalog lifecycle is how the set is governed over time.

Now put a signature on it. Work a small case:

# what a sign-off actually covered, and what it covers later
2026-09-14  finance-lead approves: action_class "refund <= 1k"
            bound to server  finance-mcp@2.3   tools: 6
            manifest digest  sha256:9f2c…a71b
            reviewed         2 tools that move money, 4 read-only

2026-11-03  vendor ships finance-mcp@2.6 (no action by you)
            tools: 9   (+ issue_credit_note, + adjust_invoice, + void_payment)
            manifest digest  sha256:4d80…ce33   ≠ approved digest
# the approval is unchanged, the reviewer was never asked,
# and three new money-moving tools are inside an approved class.

Nothing here is a bug. The vendor shipped features, the client re-enumerated as the protocol intends, and the agent gained capability inside a boundary a named human had accepted when it held six tools. The reviewer's authority — the word Article 26(2) uses — now extends over three actions they have never seen. There is no alert for this, because every component behaved correctly.

The fix is small and mechanical. At approval time, snapshot the capability set the class resolves to — tool names, input schemas, and any trust annotations — hash it, and store the digest in the approval record. Then compare on every run:

  • Digest matches — proceed, and write the digest onto the decision receipt so the record says what was possible, not only what happened.
  • Digest differs by a removal or a description change — proceed and notify. The accountable set shrank or the prose moved; log the diff.
  • Digest differs by an addition — fail closed for that action class and re-queue the sign-off. An added tool is new authority, and new authority is the thing a signature is for.

That third rule is the whole control, and it is roughly twenty lines of code against a list you already fetch. It also converts a governance claim into something testable: you can prove the approval covered the action, which no amount of attestation prose can do.

STEP 5

Sign-off is a decision with a name, a date, a basis and a digest.

"It was approved" is worthless if approval was a green button anyone could press without reading. Meaningful sign-off has four properties, and the fourth is the one STEP 4 adds:

  • A named approver with the authority to bear the consequence — singular, not a group alias that resolves to everyone.
  • A recorded basis: what they reviewed. The risk assessment, the eval results, the scope.
  • Capture in the audit trail, so the decision and its owner survive staff turnover.
  • A pinned capability digest, so the approval has a boundary that a machine can check and an expiry it announces.

Scale the structure to consequence rather than improvising per incident. Low-consequence, reversible actions run under the operator's standing accountability. As consequence rises, accountability is exercised closer to the act: operator review of exceptions, then explicit pre-action sign-off by a named approver, then dual control — the separation of duties pattern. The agent's capability may be constant; the accountability structure around it tightens as stakes rise, and the ladder is decided in advance so an incident never becomes an argument about who was supposed to be watching. Keep an eye on the ceiling: a ladder that queues more approvals than your reviewers can genuinely read has traded a governance gap for an oversight-capacity one, which looks better on paper and is not better.

STEP 6

The honest trade-off.

Explicit accountability has a cost, and pinning it has a second one. Naming people puts them on the hook, which they will reasonably resist, and it slows high-consequence paths with review. Digest-pinning adds a failure mode that did not exist before: a vendor's routine release can halt an action class until a human re-signs, which is a production stall caused by your own control. Teams feel that stall immediately and feel the unbounded sign-off never, which is why the unpinned approval keeps winning.

Price it before you reject it. In the worked case above, re-signing is one reviewer reading three tool definitions — call it fifteen minutes, at the rate the vendor ships, perhaps twice a quarter per server. Set against it the alternative: an agent exercising money-moving authority nobody reviewed, discovered after the fact, in a regime where the absence of a named owner for the action is itself the finding. And the stall is tunable — fail closed on additions only, allow removals and wording changes through with a notification, and the pager stays quiet for the common case.

Name the operator, enumerate the class, pin the digest. Concretely, this week: add an approved_capability_digest column to wherever approvals live, populate it for your highest-consequence action class by hashing the sorted tool names and schemas that class resolves to, and make an added tool fail closed. Then write the digest onto every decision record. An action no specific person owns is an action your organization owns by default, on the worst possible terms — and an action owned by a person who cannot enumerate it is the same thing with a signature on top.
