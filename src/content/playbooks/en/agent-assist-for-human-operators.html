Playbook · Voice & Realtime Agents

Agent assist: the model whispers, the human talks.

The largest peer-reviewed study of this product — Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, about 5,200 support staff — found roughly 15% more issues resolved per hour on average, 34% for novices, and close to nothing for experienced high performers. Build accordingly: the decision that moves your number is who sees suggestions, not how good the suggestions are. And because the human is in the turn-taking loop and the model is not, both of the instincts you brought from autonomous voice agents invert — your latency budget loosens and your precision requirement gets far harsher.

STEP 1

You are out of the turn loop, so stop optimising the wrong thing.

An autonomous voice agent has to produce an answer inside the gap a caller hears as a pause, which is why a stack assembled from individually fast components still ships late — the arithmetic is in the latency budget. Agent assist has no such constraint. The rep is already talking; the suggestion has to arrive before the rep needs it, not before the caller notices silence.

That buys you an enormous amount of engineering freedom, and most teams spend it badly by building the same streaming architecture anyway. What the slack is actually worth:

  • You can retrieve properly. Two seconds is enough for hybrid search, a rerank and a grounded answer, rather than the one-shot nearest-neighbour lookup retrieval in the voice loop is forced into.
  • You can verify before you show. A second pass that checks the cited passage actually supports the claim costs latency the caller never experiences. Grounding is cheap here and expensive there.
  • You can batch on utterance boundaries, not tokens. Streaming a half-formed suggestion into a rep's peripheral vision is worse than showing a complete one a beat later, because a changing panel pulls the eye twice.
  • You do not need a realtime speech model at all. Streaming ASR plus a text model is usually the better trade, since you are not generating audio — which removes most of the stack in the speech stack.

The one hard deadline that remains is the compliance nudge: a warning that the rep is about to say something they must not has to land before they say it, and that one genuinely is a realtime problem. Treat it as a separate, narrow, low-latency path with its own model, not as a priority flag on the general suggestion queue.

STEP 2

The scarce resource is the rep's attention, and every suggestion spends some.

Here is the inversion that decides the product. For an autonomous agent, a wrong answer costs you a wrong answer. For agent assist, a wrong answer costs you a wrong answer and the reading time, and so does a right answer the rep did not need. Reading is serial with listening: a human cannot parse a paragraph and track a sentence a caller is saying at the same time. So the cost of a suggestion is incurred when it is displayed, not when it is accepted.

Which means the operating point is nothing like a detector's. You are not trying to catch every moment where help would be possible; you are trying to make the shown suggestions worth the glance. Concretely:

  • Tune for precision at low recall. Four useful cards in a ten-minute call beats forty cards of which fifteen are useful. Set a confidence floor and let the rest go unsuggested — silence is a valid output and the default one.
  • One thing at a time, in one place. A fixed panel that replaces its contents costs one saccade. A feed that accumulates costs a scan on every glance, and reps stop looking at feeds within a week.
  • Never move a target the rep is reading. Suppress updates while a card is being looked at or hovered; re-ranking under someone's eyes is the fastest way to teach them to ignore the panel.
  • Make the claim and the source the same glance. A suggestion the rep has to verify elsewhere has negative value under time pressure — see citations and source attribution, and hallucination and grounding for why the citation has to be checked rather than generated.

The test that settles an argument about density: count suggestions shown per call and suggestions used per call, then compute the ratio. If reps use fewer than a quarter of what you show them, you are not running an assist product, you are running a distraction with a good hit rate buried in it. Cutting what is shown usually raises use per call in absolute terms, because the panel starts being worth looking at.

STEP 3

The published effect is concentrated, so routing beats tuning.

The study worth building on is Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, "Generative AI at Work", Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), covering roughly 5,200 customer-support staff with a conversational assistant. The headline is about 15% more issues resolved per hour (14% in the earlier working-paper version). The distribution matters far more than the mean: around 34% for novice and lower-skilled workers, and minimal effect on experienced, high-skill agents. The authors' reading is that the tool disseminates the practices of top performers to everyone else.

Take that seriously and three product decisions change:

  • Route suggestions by tenure, not by queue. Your most productive reps are the population for whom the published gain is near zero and the attention cost is not. Default them to the compliance nudge and the after-call summary, and make the suggestion panel opt-in.
  • Your best reps are the training set, not the users. If the mechanism is dissemination of top-performer practice, then mining their resolved calls is the product, and the assist is the delivery channel. That is the same loop as training on your own traces, with the unusual property that the labels are already there.
  • Expect the average to decay as it works. A tool that lifts novices toward the median shrinks its own measured effect over time as the novice cohort shrinks. A flat average after a year is not necessarily a failing tool; a flat per-cohort average is.

Vendor handle-time claims in this category — the 25–30% reductions that appear in every datasheet — are mostly traceable only to the vendor or to an analyst report commissioned by one, and none of them publish the tenure mix of the population they measured. Since the peer-reviewed effect is almost entirely a novice effect, an average from somebody else's workforce tells you nothing about yours. Treat those numbers as marketing and run the pilot per cohort.

STEP 4

Ship the surfaces in order of payoff, which is not the order of excitement.

Four surfaces, cheapest and safest first:

  • After-call work. The summary, the disposition code, the CRM fields. Zero attention cost because the call is over, a clear time saving, and errors are reviewable before they commit. This is the whole of after-call work and CRM writeback and it is where almost every deployment should start.
  • Retrieval on demand. The rep asks; the model answers with a citation. Attention is spent deliberately by the person spending it, which makes the precision requirement the rep's problem rather than your threshold's, and makes this surface forgiving.
  • Proactive answers. The model volunteers the policy or the article it thinks is relevant. This is the surface everyone demos and the one that needs the confidence floor from STEP 2, because here you are spending attention on the rep's behalf.
  • Realtime guidance and compliance. Checklists, required disclosures, a nudge when a prohibited phrase is forming. Highest value per firing and highest risk: a false nudge mid-sentence derails a human in a way a false suggestion in a panel does not. Narrow, deterministic triggers wherever possible — a required disclosure that has not been said yet is a state check, not a classification.

One surface to be careful with: live sentiment or escalation scoring shown to the rep. It is easy to build, demos well, and tends to make reps manage the score rather than the caller. If you want it, show it to the supervisor — which makes it a review queue problem rather than an in-call one, and connects to escalation and warm transfer.

STEP 5

Instrument the accept, but keep the edit — that is the whole dataset.

Agent assist has a property no autonomous agent has: a trained human adjudicates every output in real time, for free, as part of their job. Most deployments throw away nine-tenths of that by logging a boolean. The four events, in increasing value:

  • Shown — the denominator for everything, and the only measure of attention cost.
  • Ignored — a weak negative. The rep may have been busy, so do not train on it hard.
  • Used — a positive, and the one number every dashboard already has.
  • Used and changed — the gold. The diff between what you proposed and what the rep actually said is a human correction on a real case, which is the most expensive kind of label there is and here it arrives free. Store the before, the after and the call context together.

Then measure outcomes per cohort rather than in aggregate: handle time and resolution rate split by tenure band, because an average over a changing workforce mix is the one statistic guaranteed to mislead you. Fractional time savings covers the harder question of whether minutes saved become capacity or just slack, and production feedback signals covers wiring the events into an eval set. Note that if edits become your training data, the rep is now an annotator and the labelling-quality problems in annotation and labelling ops apply to them — including the one where a rep learns that accepting is faster than editing.

STEP 6

The compliance asymmetry, and the road to autonomy.

Assist sits in a genuinely easier regulatory position than an autonomous agent, and it is worth being precise about which parts relax and which do not. What relaxes: a model suggesting words to an employee who decides what to say is generally not an automated decision about the caller, and the agent-disclosure duties in disclosing the agent on a call are typically written about who the caller is talking to — and they are still talking to a person. That is the real reason assist ships first in regulated contact centres, and it is a better reason than the usual one about model quality.

What does not relax: you are streaming a caller's audio to a model, so recording consent, retention and redaction obligations apply in full — recording consent and redaction. Nor does the employment side, which teams routinely forget: realtime scoring of staff is monitoring, it is often subject to consultation requirements, and a suggestion system that silently becomes a performance metric will be resisted by the people it was built to help.

And treat the autonomy question as sequenced rather than binary. Assist gives you the one thing you need before letting a model talk to customers unsupervised: a per-intent record of how often its proposed answer survived contact with a professional. An intent where reps accept the suggestion unedited nine times in ten is a candidate for automation; one where they rewrite it half the time is a candidate for a better article. That is progressive autonomy with an unusually clean measurement, and it is a far more honest gate than a benchmark score — see evaluating voice agents for what the autonomous version then has to clear.

Start with after-call work only, for one queue, for one month. Measure handle time and wrap-up time split by tenure band, log shown / ignored / used / used-and-changed from day one, and do not put a proactive suggestion panel in front of anyone until you can state your precision at your chosen confidence floor. When you do add the panel, make it opt-in for reps above median productivity — the published evidence says they are the cohort you are most likely to slow down, and they are also the cohort whose edits are worth the most. If you only do one thing from this page: store the diff between your suggestion and what the rep actually said. That dataset is the reason to build assist even if the productivity number disappoints.
