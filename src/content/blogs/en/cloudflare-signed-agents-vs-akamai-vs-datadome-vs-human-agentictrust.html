Buy any of these four and you get the same thing: a verified cryptographic identity for the AI agents at your door. Which is why the purchase decision is not about verification at all. A 2026 preprint pointed seven AI browsing agents at Cloudflare and found it fully blocked exactly one — the one that honestly declares itself a verified bot. Verification binds the agents that cooperate. What you are actually buying is the vocabulary you get to speak afterwards, and only two of the four can say "browse, but do not check out".

The standard they all implement, and what its authors say it does not do

Two documents get conflated constantly, including by vendors. RFC 9421, HTTP Message Signatures, is finished — a Proposed Standard published in February 2024, with mature libraries everywhere. Web Bot Auth is the thin profile on top: draft-ietf-webbotauth-httpsig-protocol, whose latest revision we could find is -01, "HTTP Message Signatures for automated traffic", dated 29 October 2026. The working group was chartered in October 2025 and adopted the draft in September 2026. It is not an RFC and no publication date is set. Anyone telling you to "implement the standard" is a revision or two ahead of the documents; our deep-dive on the protocol covers the signing machinery.

What matters commercially is a passage in the draft itself, which is unusually candid for a specification. A valid signature, it says, "says nothing about who operates the Agent, whether the Agent is benign, or whether the request is authorized". The protocol does not authenticate human users, and it does not define authorization or delegation. The original individual draft was authored out of Cloudflare, so this is not a critic's framing — it is the vendors' own.

That sentence is the whole market. Every one of these four products verifies identity to specification. None of them can derive authority from it. The authority has to come from policy you write, and the four give you radically different languages to write it in.

How much traffic is even signed

Less than the category implies, and the honest figures are self-published rather than peer-reviewed. One October 2026 probe of 150 AI crawler operators found 55 publishing Web Bot Auth keys; a 75-host survey in September 2026 found 17 serving a directory with at least one key and 47 returning no directory at all. One vendor outside this comparison reported that only 2.3% of AI agent sessions it classified as legitimate carried a verified signature. We could not trace these to primary datasets, so hold the specific percentages loosely — but every independent probe we found points the same way, and none of the four vendors publishes a contradicting number.

There is a quieter trap in the same area. An open-source Web Bot Auth verifier's own README warns that verification "is easy to get subtly wrong, and each mistake becomes a bypass", and admits it does not verify the signature on a key-directory response. The weakness in this scheme has never been the signature; it is key lookup and verifier implementation. That is an argument for buying verification rather than writing it, which is the strongest case any of these four can make.

Cloudflare — the directory, and the only meter

Cloudflare got here first. Signed agents arrived on 28 August 2025 as an extension of the existing Verified Bots programme, with a launch cohort of ChatGPT agent, Block's Goose, Browserbase and Anchor Browser. An operator self-serves through a Bot Submission Form in the dashboard and, on acceptance, appears in BotBase and the public Radar bots-and-agents directory. An agent can be on the signed-agents list or the verified-bots list, not both.

Two details reward attention. Cloudflare "reserves the right to re-assign Verified bot categories if the bot's public documentation and observed behavior differ from the category listed in the bot submission form" — the label is revocable and behaviour-conditioned, which is more than most allowlists admit. And from 1 July 2026 BotBase carries a Direct versus Intermediary access field, distinguishing an agent acting on a user's behalf in the moment from one fetching for later use. That distinction is the beginning of the thing the signature cannot give you, and it is metadata rather than policy.

Enforcement is category-level: a verified or signed agent becomes a classification you use in security and firewall rules. That is powerful and coarse. You can refuse a category on a path; you cannot tell a single agent that reading is fine and purchasing is not. Zones can also ask Cloudflare to disable verification entirely for their zone, which is worth knowing exists.

Cloudflare alone charges for the traffic. Pay per crawl bills per successful content retrieval, with a floor its own documentation puts at $0.001 per crawl, a crawler-price response header for per-URL overrides, a crawler-max-price request header on the crawler side, and an HTTP 402 Payment Required for crawlers that have not paid. Note the number: multiple third-party write-ups state the floor is $0.01, ten times higher. Cloudflare's docs are the stronger source. It remains a closed beta.

On volume, Cloudflare's chairman said on 3 June 2026 that automated traffic had passed human traffic, at roughly 57% of requests to page content per Cloudflare Radar. A separate Radar-derived figure of 35.49% for Q3 2026 uses "all HTTP requests" as its denominator; the two are not comparable and should not be blended.

Akamai — the enterprise default, the coarsest vocabulary

Akamai states that Web Bot Auth "is already supported by Akamai's bot & abuse solutions, inclusive of bot visibility and mitigation in Akamai App & API Protector". We could not establish when: the blog post carries no date, one third-party citation puts it at November 2025, and listing metadata disagreed with that. Treat the capability as present and the date as unknown. Operators register through a self-serve form submitting agent identity, user-agent string and public key; a valid signature files the request as validated-bot traffic in Akamai's bot directory, for which we could not confirm a public product name or a published list.

Once traffic is validated, site owners can allow, challenge, delay or block it. Four verbs on a traffic class, and we found no evidence of per-action rules. "Delay" is more interesting than it looks — a tarpit is a real tool against scrapers whose economics depend on throughput, and none of the other three advertise it. But for anyone trying to govern agentic commerce, four verbs is the wrong shape: it cannot express the distinction between an agent reading a product page and an agent completing a purchase.

Akamai is building outward instead of downward, pairing Web Bot Auth with Know Your Agent and the Visa Trusted Agent Protocol, and has announced an agentic security framework for Bot & Agent Control organised around identity, authentication, trust analysis and edge enforcement. Pricing is quote-only with no published list rate.

Akamai is also the one vendor publishing a number that cuts against the prevailing narrative. Its July 2026 State of the Internet report found 47.9% of all AI bot traffic across its network between July and December 2025 was in the commerce vertical, with AI training crawlers accounting for more than 70% of AI bot triggers there — and its January 2026 AI Pulse reported overall AI bot traffic up 185 million requests a day, a 1.42× increase, while agentic AI bot traffic had declined. A secondary summary restates the 47.9% as a share of all commerce traffic, which it is not; that misreading is circulating.

DataDome — verification on by default, policy per endpoint

DataDome has the strongest tier claim of the four, and it is one sentence: "Web Bot Auth signature verification is live now for all DataDome customers. No setup required." No plan gate, no opt-in. It accepts five verification methods in all — signatures per RFC 9421, two-way reverse DNS, a static IP list, a published dynamic IP list, and a private autonomous system — and is explicit that the trust it assigns depends on which method the agent chose. Unauthenticated automation is blocked by default. Operators request addition to the catalog through a self-service portal.

The catalog is also visibly maintained, which is rarer than it sounds. When Meta's Muse agent switched to signing as a muse.ai key directory, DataDome published that it had added the new directory to its list. That is the unglamorous work this whole category rests on, and it is the first thing to go stale at a vendor that has lost interest.

Enforcement is where DataDome separates from Cloudflare and Akamai. Its Agent Trust Management product documents per-endpoint policies "applying different rules depending on the type of action the agent is attempting", with the worked example of an agent allowed to browse and check out but blocked from creating accounts. The product page offers full access, specific paths, or a block, "per agent type, per endpoint, per business policy", plus a 100-point Agent Trust score and custom rules over 60-plus traffic attributes.

One vendor claim to treat carefully: DataDome states that 80% of AI agents do not properly identify themselves when visiting your site. No methodology is given, and the figure sits on a page selling the remedy.

HUMAN — the only one that treats a read as an action

HUMAN's AgenticTrust, a feature of HUMAN Sightline (announced 30 July 2025), assigns High trust "when we can verify the agent via cryptographic key on each request", with Medium and Low levels computed from how verified the agent is and how spoofable it is. We could not establish when RFC 9421 verification specifically shipped, and — unlike the other three — we found no public directory of known-good agents and no operator registration route at all. For an agent builder trying to get allowlisted, that is a real gap. For a site owner, it may not be one.

Because the enforcement model is the most precise of the four, and one documented detail is genuinely different in kind. HUMAN defines discrete permissions, including a separate Checkout permission covering "purchasing items from the site, including as a guest user". Then: "if an agent has only the basic content permission, AgenticTrust will block the agent from navigating to or reading pages related to the remaining permissions such as login or checkout pages."

Read that again. The permission governs reading the checkout page, not merely submitting the purchase. Every other model here treats a GET as neutral and gates the write. HUMAN gates reconnaissance. Whether that is right depends on your threat model — an agent that cannot read the checkout page also cannot report the shipping cost to its user — but it is the only product in this comparison that models the read as part of the capability.

Two caveats. All of it is HUMAN's own documentation; we found no independent test that the checkout block holds in practice. And the frequently repeated claim that browse-but-block-checkout is unique to HUMAN is false — DataDome documents an equivalent control.

The numbers that should temper the purchase

HUMAN publishes the most useful data in the category, and two figures matter. Its Satori threat-intelligence team reports that 1 in every 18 requests using an AI crawler user agent is fake, across traffic associated with 16 well-known AI crawlers, with ChatGPT-User spoofed 16.7% of the time. That is the case for verification in a single statistic.

And then the deflating one: payment and checkout attempts by agents "more than doubled", from under 1% of traffic in October to over 2% in late November. Over 2%. The entire agentic-commerce control surface being sold across this category currently governs roughly one request in fifty. Buy for the trajectory if you like, but price it as a bet on the trajectory, not as a response to current volume.

Cross-cutting: verification constrains the cooperative

The finding that should reframe this whole market is academic rather than commercial. A preprint titled FP-Agent: Fingerprinting AI Browsing Agents tested seven AI browsing agents. Its own fingerprinting detected all seven. Cloudflare fully blocked exactly one — and the paper notes that agent appears in Cloudflare's bot directory as a verified bot, inferring that the successful block rested on self-identification. Six agents that did not declare themselves went through.

Set that against the vendor framing and the asymmetry is stark. Every one of these four products is, in its detection half, a mechanism for applying policy to agents that volunteer who they are. That is not worthless — a 1-in-18 spoof rate means the volunteers need protecting from impersonators, and all four do that well. But a control that binds the honest is a compliance instrument, not a security boundary, and the marketing for all four blurs the distinction. The uncooperative agent is handled, where it is handled at all, by behavioural fingerprinting that none of these vendors claims is complete.

Which is why we could find no documented case of the same named agent being verified by one of these four and blocked by another. The interesting disagreements are not between vendors; they are between a vendor and an operator. Cloudflare estimated Perplexity's undeclared crawling at 3–6 million requests a day across tens of thousands of domains against 20–25 million from its declared agent, and Perplexity disputed the characterisation. That is the real contested surface, and no signature settles it.

One operational caveat worth checking on your own stack: Google's documentation is reported to state that it does not sign every request from a given agent. If true, any allowlist that treats unsigned Google traffic as unverified will refuse legitimate requests, and any rule that treats signed-or-nothing as a clean partition is wrong on a major operator. Verify it against Google's own docs before writing the rule either way.

Whichever you pick, the sequencing is the same. Buy verification because getting it right yourself is the documented failure mode. Then write the policy that the signature explicitly does not give you, and write it in terms of actions rather than agents — because the agent names will churn every quarter and the list of things you will not let a stranger do will not.

FAQ

Is Web Bot Auth a standard yet?

No. The underlying RFC 9421 (HTTP Message Signatures) is a Proposed Standard from February 2024, but Web Bot Auth itself is an IETF working-group draft — the latest revision we found is -01, dated 29 October 2026 — adopted in September 2026 with no publication date set.

Does a verified signature mean the agent is authorised to act?

No, and the draft says so explicitly: a valid signature "says nothing about who operates the Agent, whether the Agent is benign, or whether the request is authorized". It names the operator's software. Authorisation and delegation are out of scope, so they have to come from policy you write.

Which of the four can block checkout while allowing browsing?

DataDome and HUMAN. DataDome documents per-endpoint rules keyed to the type of action; HUMAN defines a discrete Checkout permission and, notably, blocks an under-permissioned agent from even reading checkout or login pages. Cloudflare works at bot-category level and Akamai offers allow, challenge, delay or block.

Can an agent spoof its way past these?

Not the signature — we found no published break of it. The documented weaknesses are elsewhere: verifier implementation and key-directory lookup, and the fact that an agent which simply never declares itself is handled by behavioural fingerprinting rather than by verification. A 2026 preprint found Cloudflare fully blocked only the one of seven tested browsing agents that self-identifies as verified.

How much agent checkout traffic is there actually?

By HUMAN's measurement, payment and checkout attempts rose from under 1% of traffic in October 2026 to over 2% in late November. Treat purchases in this category as a bet on growth rather than a response to present volume.

Does any of them charge AI companies for access?

Only Cloudflare, through pay per crawl: billed per successful retrieval, with a floor its documentation puts at $0.001 per crawl, a crawler-price header for per-URL pricing and an HTTP 402 for crawlers that have not paid. Several third-party sources state $0.01; Cloudflare's own docs are the better source.
