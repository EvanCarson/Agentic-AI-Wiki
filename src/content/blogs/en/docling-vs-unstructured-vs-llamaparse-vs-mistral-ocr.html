The same thousand pages cost nothing or about ninety dollars depending on which of these four you point at them — two orders of magnitude for one input, which tells you the products are not substitutes. And the accuracy figure every comparison leads with is character-level, which is not what breaks retrieval: a parser that reads every glyph correctly and flattens a table into prose has destroyed the answer while scoring beautifully. Choose on structure fidelity and on where the compute runs, because those are the two decisions you cannot reverse later without re-processing your whole corpus.

The question before the question

Every one of these tools will extract the text from a clean single-column PDF, and if that is your corpus you should pick the cheapest and stop reading. The interesting failures live in the 20% of documents that carry most of the value: a financial statement where the numbers only mean something in relation to their row and column headers, a two-column paper where naïve extraction interleaves the columns into nonsense, a scanned form where the label and the value are spatially adjacent and semantically inseparable.

What breaks in those cases is not character recognition. It is structure: reading order, table cell topology, and whether a heading arrives as a heading or as one more line of body text. Reading order failures corrupt the chunk boundary, so the chunk you retrieve contains half of two unrelated thoughts. Table flattening destroys the row–column relationship, and a language model reading a flattened table will confidently attribute the wrong number to the wrong year. Lost heading hierarchy removes the only signal a structure-aware chunker has to work from, which quietly turns your careful chunking strategy into fixed-size splitting.

This is why table metrics in the document-parsing literature use TEDS — tree edit distance based similarity — rather than a character score: it compares the extracted table as a tree against the ground-truth tree, so collapsing a table into a paragraph is scored as the catastrophe it is. OmniDocBench, the benchmark most of these tools report against, covers 1,651 PDF pages spanning ten document types, five layout types and five languages, precisely because parser performance does not transfer across document mixes.

Docling — deep dive

What it owns

Docling came out of IBM Research, is MIT-licensed, and is now hosted by the LF AI & Data Foundation — a governance detail that matters more than it sounds, because it is the difference between a permissive licence and a permissive licence with a foundation standing behind it. It is past 64,000 GitHub stars and ships releases at a pace that makes pinning a version part of your job rather than an optional nicety. Its input list has also grown well past PDFs — Office formats, HTML, EPUB, email, images, LaTeX, ODF, even audio and video — so the old shorthand that Docling is the narrow option and Unstructured the broad one no longer holds.

What you get back

Not Markdown, primarily — a DoclingDocument, a typed tree with sections, tables, captions and figures as first-class nodes. This is the single most underrated thing on this list. A tree is what a structure-aware chunker needs; Markdown is a lossy serialization of a tree that you then have to re-parse to recover the same information. If you are going to chunk on headings or keep a table intact through retrieval, starting from a tree instead of from text saves you writing a second parser.

What it makes hard

You are now operating an ML pipeline: model weights to cache, a GPU decision to make, a version to pin, and throughput that is yours to tune. The per-page fee is zero and the ops bill is not — for a one-off corpus of 50,000 pages that trade is obviously right, and for 500 pages a month it is obviously wrong. Docling also does not lead on forms and handwriting, so a corpus of scanned handwritten intake forms is not its fight.

Unstructured — deep dive

What it owns

Being a service rather than a library. The Apache-2.0 package will partition PDFs, Office documents, email, HTML and images behind one interface, with a per-document strategy knob so you spend OCR only where OCR is needed — but the thing you are usually buying is the hosted platform that runs that pipeline, with connectors, and with someone else on call for it. That is a legitimate product, and it is a different purchase from a library.

What you get back

A flat list of typed elements — Title, NarrativeText, Table, ListItem — each carrying metadata: page number, coordinates, parent element id. The parent id means the hierarchy is recoverable, but you are reconstructing it rather than being handed it, which is a real difference in code you have to write and test.

What it makes hard

Working out what you are paying. The spread inside their own catalogue is the thing to notice: the serverless API starts around $1 per thousand pages while pay-as-you-go runs around $0.03 a page — thirty times more for the same document, depending which door you walked through. Choose the door deliberately. On the technical side, the flat element list means the hierarchy is yours to rebuild, and table fidelity is where their own published benchmark is most candid: 100% on simple tables and 75% on complex ones. That second number is the one that predicts your financial-statement corpus.

LlamaParse — deep dive

What it owns

Letting a vision model look at the page. On the layouts that defeat rule-based extraction — dense tables, multi-column academic papers, slide decks where reading order is a visual judgment — a model that sees the rendering has information a text extractor does not. It also holds latency roughly flat across document sizes, which matters when parsing sits in a user-facing path rather than a nightly batch.

What you get back

Markdown per page, with tables rendered as Markdown or HTML, plus optional extraction directly against a JSON shape you supply — useful when you know what you are looking for and want the structure enforced on the way out rather than parsed back in afterwards.

What it makes hard

The mode is the price and the price is the argument. From roughly $0.003 a page at the cheap end to roughly $0.09 in agentic modes with top-tier models is a 30× internal spread, and the mode that wins your benchmark is very often the expensive one. Two consequences: a proof-of-concept run in the top mode will not survive its own economics at production volume, and a cost estimate that does not name the mode is not an estimate. Everything is hosted, so every page leaves your network — a fact your data-residency review will care about more than your retrieval metrics do.

Mistral OCR 4 — deep dive

What it owns

Geometry and calibration. Launched in June 2026, it returns a structured representation of the page: paragraph-level bounding boxes, block classifications, and confidence scores per block, across 170 languages. The confidence score is the differentiator and it is oddly rare — it is the one output on this page that lets you build a routing rule instead of a hope.

What you get back

Blocks with coordinates, types and confidence. Coordinates let you cite a source region rather than a page, which is the difference between "this claim came from page 14" and a highlight a reader can check in one glance. That is a real product feature for anything doing grounded answers over documents.

What it makes hard

It is OCR-shaped, not document-model-shaped: you get an excellent description of what is on the page and less help turning it into a document tree, so the reassembly work lands on you. At $4 per thousand pages standard, $2 in batch and $5 annotated, it sits mid-pack on price — cheaper than a top LlamaParse mode by more than an order of magnitude, and a real line item next to self-hosting.

Cross-cutting comparison

What each one is really selling

Set side by side on the same axis, the four separate cleanly — and not along the lines the marketing suggests. Docling sells control: of the compute, the model versions and the upgrade schedule, charged in operational work. Unstructured sells the opposite of control, and means to: an Apache-2.0 library exists, but the product is a hosted pipeline with connectors and someone else on call. LlamaParse and Mistral OCR both sell you a hosted model per page and differ in what they hand back — LlamaParse aims at a finished Markdown document, Mistral OCR at an accurate description of the physical page. Read that way, the pair you are actually choosing between is usually Docling versus one hosted option, not four tools.

The published rankings disagree, and that is the finding

Go looking for a winner and you will find several. One 2026 comparison ranks Unstructured first, LlamaParse second and Docling third, with Mistral OCR well down the list. Another reports Docling at 97.9% on complex table extraction. Unstructured's own benchmark page reports 100% on simple tables and 75% on complex ones — a gap that tells you more than either number. On OmniDocBench, purpose-built OCR-plus-table pipelines score around 82 TEDS on table structure while general vision-language models land nearer 71–74, and separate evaluations note that reading-order accuracy degrades for every method as layouts get harder.

These results are not reconcilable, and trying is a mistake. They are measurements of different document mixes, several of them published by a party with an interest in the outcome, and parser performance is a function of your documents in a way that model benchmarks are not. The only ranking that predicts your outcome is the one you produce on fifty of your own worst pages — and building it is a day of work, not a research project: take fifty documents your users actually complain about, hand-write the ground-truth tables, and score each parser's tables with TEDS. Everything above is a prior for which two you should test first.

The decision that survives, and the one that does not

Price and quality both move: modes get added, prices get cut, a new model release reshuffles the accuracy table within a quarter. Two things do not move on that timescale. The first is locus — whether the compute runs on your hardware — because unwinding that means re-architecting an ingest path, not editing a config. The second is the shape of what you store. If your pipeline commits to flat text now, adding structure later means re-processing everything you have ever ingested, at whatever the price is then; if it commits to a tree, a parser swap is a swap. That asymmetry is the reason to pick on output shape rather than on this month's benchmark, and it is the same asymmetry that makes an embedding-model change so expensive.

FAQ

Is a vision-language model always better than a rule-based parser?

No, and the cases where it loses are predictable. On clean, consistently formatted documents a layout-model pipeline is faster, cheaper and more deterministic, and it will not hallucinate a plausible number into a cell it could not read. Vision models earn their price on layouts where reading order is a judgment call.

Does the per-page price really matter next to model inference costs?

It matters at ingest volume, not at query volume. Parsing is a one-time cost per document but the corpus is often large — a million pages at $0.09 is $90,000, at $0.002 it is $2,000, and the same corpus self-hosted is a week of GPU time. It also matters every time you re-index, which you will do more often than you expect.

How careful do I need to be about licences here?

More careful than the badge on the repository suggests, and the care belongs in the dependency tree rather than at the top level. Docling is MIT and Unstructured is Apache-2.0, both unremarkable — but a document parser pulls in OCR engines, PDF renderers and vision models with their own terms, and a filed (now closed) issue on the Unstructured repository pointed at exactly this: transitive dependencies under AGPL-3.0-or-later and LGPL sitting under an Apache-2.0 package. Whichever self-hosted parser you choose, run a licence scan on the installed extras you actually enable, not on the repository you cloned.

What about Marker, MinerU, Reducto, or a cloud provider's document AI?

All live in the same space, and Marker and MinerU in particular belong on the self-hosted shortlist next to Docling. The four here were chosen to span the axes — self-hosted versus hosted, breadth versus depth, document tree versus page geometry — not to claim they are the only options.

Can I mix parsers in one pipeline?

Yes, and it is often the right answer: a cheap path for the 80% of documents that are clean, and an expensive path for the rest. This is the single best use of a per-block confidence score — route on it rather than on file type, and the expensive parser only sees pages that need it.

How much does chunking strategy depend on the parser?

Almost entirely. Structure-aware chunking needs structure to be aware of, so a parser that loses heading hierarchy silently downgrades your chunker to fixed-size splitting. If you are choosing a parser and a chunking strategy at the same time, choose the parser first.
