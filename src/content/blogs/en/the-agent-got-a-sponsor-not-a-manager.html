Ignite 2026 looked like Microsoft giving agents a job — an identity, a mailbox, a calendar, a slot in the org chart — and the write-ups duly reported that each one comes with an accountable manager. It does not. The relationship Entra Agent ID will not create an agent without is the sponsor, and the sponsor is deliberately the weakest of the three it defines: a sponsor can switch an agent off and cannot reconfigure it. Microsoft made the accountable field mandatory and the powerful field optional, which inverts how service accounts have worked for thirty years, and it is the part of this worth copying whether or not you ever touch Entra.

At a glance

Entra Agent ID defines three administrative relationships on an agent. Only one of them is required, and it is not the one with the most authority. Read the last column first — what each role is barred from doing is where the design lives.

RelationshipRequired?Can doCannot do
Sponsor Yes — at creation, for an agent identity and for a blueprint Disable the identity, change its sponsors, soft-delete it Change application or authentication settings; re-enable; restore
Owner No (one blueprint page calls it recommended) Setup, configuration, credentials; re-enable, restore, hard-delete Nothing within the object it is assigned to
Manager No Organisational oversight; request access packages; see the agents reporting to them Modify or delete the agent at all

Two framing notes before the argument. First, Ignite 2026 ran 17–20 November 2026; the agent-mailbox feature is Microsoft 365 Resources for Agents, and its service description lists public-preview dates of 17 November 2026 for Autopilot agents and 1 December 2026 for the Sales Development Agent, Service Agent and Copilot Studio. Several aggregators dated the news 19 November and added the accountable-manager line; neither detail survives contact with Microsoft's own pages. Second, "manager" is not fiction — it is one of the three named relationships — but in Microsoft Graph the manager relationship hangs off the agent's optional paired user account, not off the agent identity that governance actually targets.

The mandatory field is the one with no configure button

A sponsor is described as the business representative accountable for an agent's purpose and lifecycle decisions, without technical administrative access. Read that second clause as a feature. The sponsor's permitted actions are a short, closed list of non-destructive lifecycle operations: disable the identity, change who sponsors it, soft-delete it. They cannot touch application settings on an identity or a blueprint. They cannot alter authentication properties. And they cannot re-enable or restore anything they removed — that is reserved for owners and administrators.

Set that against how every service account you have ever inherited was governed. The only human reliably recorded against it was whoever held the credentials, and the field that mattered was an optional description that said migration script, do not delete. Accountability was a wiki page; administration was the schema. Entra Agent ID reverses the pair: the accountable human is a required attribute checked when the object is created, and the human who can change the agent's authentication is optional.

This is separation of duties expressed in a directory schema rather than in a policy document, and the asymmetry runs the right way. The person who answers for what the agent does gets a stop button and no configure button. The person who can reconfigure it is a separate, optional assignment. If one human needs both, they hold both roles explicitly, which is a decision somebody made rather than a side effect of who happened to run the deployment script.

The constraint is doing work even at the edges: Microsoft caps sponsors at 100 per object with no more than five of those being groups, and excludes role-assignable and assigned-membership security groups. A field that could be satisfied by All Employees would be a field that is always technically populated and never actually accountable.

A sponsor's powers are exactly the kill-switch set

Line up what a sponsor may do — disable, re-sponsor, soft-delete — and you have described a kill switch plus a handover, and nothing else. The accountable human can always stop the agent. They can always hand the accountability on. They cannot make the agent do anything new, and they cannot quietly undo the stop.

That last restriction is the one that looks like friction and is not. Because a sponsor cannot re-enable a disabled identity, "this agent was switched off" is a state only an owner or an administrator can leave — which makes it a durable, attributable event rather than a toggle someone flips back before standup. The default inactivity policy says the same thing in its own words: once an agent identity has been disabled or deleted, only an owner or administrator can recover it, and sponsors are explicitly not authorised to do so. An accountable human with a one-way stop is a stronger control than an accountable human with a reversible one, for the same reason that a fire alarm you cannot silence yourself is a better alarm.

It also makes the stop cheap, which is the precondition everyone skips. A reviewer who has to open a ticket to pause a misbehaving agent will instead watch it for another hour. Giving the accountable party a direct disable, while keeping restore away from them, is the shape that lets you lower the cost of stopping without raising the cost of being wrong — the same trade the reversibility argument makes about product actions, applied to an identity.

Microsoft also shipped the decay curve, which is the honest part

A required sponsor stops an agent being born unaccountable. Nothing stops it becoming unaccountable, because people change teams and leave companies, and Microsoft has clearly looked at that. The default inactivity policy notifies sponsors and owners at 60 days of inactivity, disables the identity at 90 days and deletes it at 455 — and on the default policy those actions and timeframes are not customisable. When no sponsor or owner is present, the notice goes to fallback recipients the administrator supplied, which is a quiet admission that the required field can end up pointing at nobody.

The admin-centre side is blunter still. Agent Registry shows an Agents without owners count with a one-click filter to isolate them, updated as users are deleted, and Microsoft states plainly that shared agents become ownerless when you delete the user who created them. Reassignment is narrower than the dashboard suggests: you can reassign ownership of shared Agent Builder and Copilot Studio agents and not of first-party agents, a Reassign Ownerless Agents rule can bulk-transfer to the previous owner's manager via the Entra hierarchy but supports only agents built with Copilot Agent Builder, and Microsoft's own documentation says administrators must currently identify and transfer ownership manually, which can result in lifecycle governance gaps.

For sponsors specifically, the leaver path is a Lifecycle Workflows task that finds the departing user's manager, adds them as sponsor on each agent identity the leaver sponsored, and removes the leaver — which requires the leaver's own manager attribute to be populated. That is the dependency nobody audits. An organisation with patchy manager attributes has a sponsorship-transfer mechanism that silently has nowhere to send things, and it will discover this at the worst moment, which is a reorganisation.

Nobody builds a dedicated count, a dedicated filter, a minimum-sponsor policy and a 455-day timer for a problem they believe is rare. Microsoft publishes no figure for how common ownerless agents are in real tenants — the existence of purpose-built tooling for finding them is the finding.

What to copy if you are not on Entra

Most teams running agents in November 2026 are not going to adopt an identity platform because of this, and do not need to. The transferable part is three decisions and one asymmetry, and they cost a migration and an afternoon.

Make the accountable human a required column, validated at creation. Your agent registry probably has a nullable owner that is populated for the agents someone cared about. A required field checked when the row is written is a different object: it makes "who answers for this agent?" a query rather than an investigation, and it makes the answer's absence a build failure instead of an archaeology project.

Split accountable from administrative, and let the accountable role be the weaker one. The accountable human needs to stop the agent, hand accountability on, and nothing else. Resist the instinct to give them edit rights so they can "fix it" — the moment they can reconfigure the agent, the stop is no longer evidence of anything, because they can also un-stop it.

Build the leaver path before you need it, and test the attribute it depends on. Whatever your equivalent of "transfer to the leaver's manager" is, it reads some field in your HR or directory system. Query that field's null rate today. Then decide what happens for the nulls, because "notify a fallback recipient" is the answer Microsoft arrived at and it is better than the alternative, which is nothing happening at all.

And the asymmetry worth keeping: accountability binds at creation, and it decays continuously. Everything above is about the second half. An access review that re-asks "is this person still the right one?" on a cadence — Microsoft's own best-practice guidance suggests a quarterly sweep for missing sponsors and stale metadata, with sponsors attesting every six to twelve months — is what keeps the required field honest after the day it was filled in. Decommissioning is the other end of the same pipe.

If you take one thing: the governable unit is not the agent, it is the pair (agent, named human who can stop it). Entra enforces that pair at creation and then spends four separate features trying to keep it true. Your registry can enforce the same pair this week with a NOT NULL constraint and a weekly job that lists the rows where the human has left.

FAQ

Is Microsoft Entra Agent ID generally available?

The Entra release notes list the Agent ID platform as generally available under an April 2026 heading. Several things around it are not: blueprint instance modes, Agent ID lifecycle policies, the default inactivity policy and access packages for digital teammates are all documented as preview as of November 2026, so "Agent ID is GA" should not be read as "the governance surface is GA".

Can an agent have a manager, or not?

It can, but the manager attaches to the agent's optional paired user account rather than to the agent identity, and Microsoft Graph lists manager as a relationship on the agent-user resource. A manager gets organisational oversight and can request access packages for their agents; Microsoft states that managers have no authorisation to modify or delete agents, which owners, sponsors or administrators are required to do.

Does every Microsoft Foundry agent get its own Entra Agent ID?

Not uniformly, and this is the accountability hole worth watching. Published and hosted Foundry agents get their own Entra agent identity, newly created agents are documented as receiving a unique blueprint and identity by default, and connected agents now receive an Agent ID automatically — but unpublished or in-development agents in the same project have been documented as sharing a common project identity, and Foundry Agent Applications are documented as not registered in the Entra agent registry. Draft agents are the ones to ask about.

Does anyone else require an accountable human on an agent?

Okta is the closest. Okta for AI Agents registers agents in Universal Directory with a named human owner, assigned to establish accountability for access requests and certification reviews, and Okta's Gemini Enterprise integration describes agents tied to a human owner. We could not confirm from Okta's developer documentation that the field is mandatory at registration the way Entra's sponsor is, and found no Google or AWS documentation defining a required accountable-human attribute — the closest is an architecture principle, not an enforced schema field.

What is the single change that buys the most here?

Making the accountable field non-nullable in whatever already lists your agents. Every other control on this page — the stop button, the leaver path, the decay timer, the review cadence — assumes that field has a real human in it, and none of them degrade gracefully when it does not.
