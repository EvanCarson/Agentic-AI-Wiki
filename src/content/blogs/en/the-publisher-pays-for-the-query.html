Every scheme for the agentic web so far has argued about who gets paid when an AI reads your site. Cloudflare's Search for Agents, shipped on 25 November 2026, quietly answers a different question: who pays when an AI asks. The answer is you. One toggle turns your domain into a retrieval API, three meters start running against your account on 1 December, and not one assistant has publicly committed to calling the endpoint.

What the toggle does

Enabling it from the zone's Quick Actions (or the API) creates an ordinary Cloudflare AI Search instance in your own account, named search-for-agents-<ZONE_ID>, with your domain as its data source. AI Search is the product formerly called AutoRAG, and its crawler now identifies itself as Cloudflare-AI-Search, renamed from Cloudflare-AutoRAG in February 2026. Nothing underneath this is new; the novelty is the packaging.

The discovery mechanism is four meta tags

Cloudflare injects agent:instructions, agent:endpoint, agent:method and agent:documentation into the head of every HTML page it serves for the zone. On Cloudflare's own developer docs, the reference deployment, agent:endpoint reads https://developers.cloudflare.com/.well-known/search?q={query} and agent:method reads GET. The agent:instructions tag carries prose aimed at the model: "Use this search API to find relevant content across this site with keywords or natural-language queries…". If you have also enabled Markdown for Agents, the same four fields appear as YAML front matter under an agent: key for requests sent with Accept: text/markdown.

Two things are worth noticing about that design. It is a meta tag, not a link rel — so an agent has to fetch and parse a page before it learns the API exists, which means the first request is always the expensive one. And the endpoint also accepts the HTTP QUERY method with a plain-text or JSON body, which is a draft method co-authored at Cloudflare; using it is a bet on an agent's HTTP client supporting a method most do not.

It returns chunks, and that is the right choice

The endpoint hands back Markdown excerpts from matching pages, each with a relevance score. It does not generate an answer. The calling agent's own model writes the prose. This is the single best decision in the product: it keeps the publisher out of the business of being an LLM, keeps attribution attached to each fragment, and makes the output composable with whatever else the agent retrieved. Compare the failure mode of the alternative, where a site answers on your behalf and you own the hallucination.

How many excerpts come back is unresolved. New instances are reported to default to ten chunks; independent testers querying Cloudflare's own docs instance report fifty results per query, around 45–57 KB of Markdown or roughly 10,000–12,000 tokens, including off-topic hits. Both numbers circulate and we could not reconcile them from Cloudflare's published material. If you turn this on, measure it yourself before you reason about anyone's context budget, including your own bill.

The money runs the wrong way, and that is the story

Cloudflare's announcement is explicit that three meters apply: ingesting content, storing the index, and serving queries, following AI Search pricing and subject to included usage on Workers Free and Paid plans. Billing for AI Search was originally set for 1 November 2026 and then postponed; Cloudflare's changelog of 2 November moved it to 1 December 2026, in the same entry that changed the default sync interval from six hours to twenty-four. So the meter on this feature starts three days from the time of writing.

The published AI Search rates, beyond the free allowance, are around $0.75 per million tokens ingested, $2.00 per GB-month of storage, $0.75 per 1,000 semantic-hybrid queries and $0.10 per 1,000 full-text queries, with a monthly free allowance reported at 5 million ingestion tokens, 10 GB-month of storage and 1,000 queries of each kind. The ingestion figure is the firmest; the others we saw only in documentation summaries, so treat the exact cents as indicative and the direction as the finding.

Because the direction is unambiguous. The agent pays Cloudflare nothing to ask. The publisher pays to be asked. The New Stack's coverage put it in a headline — site owners foot the query bill — and that is a genuine inversion of everything Cloudflare has spent two years building. This is the company that shipped one-click AI-bot blocking, then pay-per-crawl with a floor its own documentation puts at $0.001 per successful crawl and a 402 Payment Required for crawlers that will not pay. The whole pitch was that AI companies should pay publishers for content. Search for Agents has publishers paying to serve AI companies, and the announcement does not mention pay-per-crawl at all.

The cap you do not have

Put the billing direction next to the access model and the hole is obvious. /.well-known/search is a public, unauthenticated GET endpoint on your domain, advertised in your own HTML, metered per query. We could find no documented rate limit or per-zone query cap for it. That is not a claim that none exists — it is a claim that Cloudflare has not published one, which is exactly the sentence you do not want to read before switching on a billable endpoint.

The mitigation is in the announcement, phrased as reassurance: usage is "subject to your domain settings (e.g. your bot management settings…)". Your existing bot management, AI Crawl Control and WAF rules still apply to the path. Read that twice, because it cuts both ways. It means you can rate-limit and scope the endpoint with tools you already own — and it means a zone with aggressive AI-bot blocking can enable this feature and then block the agents it just invited. Cloudflare did not document how its managed bot rules treat /.well-known/search by default. Check it on your own zone rather than assuming.

The demand side is entirely unsigned

Here is the part that should decide how much effort you spend on this. Not one agent, assistant or crawler has been published as a consumer of /.well-known/search. Not OpenAI, not Anthropic, not Google, not Microsoft, and not Cloudflare on behalf of a partner. Reputable coverage and independent hands-on write-ups both note it, and one proposes the obvious test: whether an assistant finds the interface without being told the URL.

Nor is the path a standard. RFC 8615 defines the /.well-known/ prefix and delegates suffixes to an IANA registry; search does not appear in the registry-derived listings we could reach. So this is a vendor-chosen path under a standardised prefix — perfectly legal, and carrying no interoperability guarantee whatsoever. An agent that supports it supports Cloudflare's convention, not a specification.

The precedent is not encouraging, and it is close at hand. llms.txt is the same shape of bet: a publisher-side file that agents were supposed to find. Adoption numbers vary wildly with the population sampled — roughly 10% of a 300,000-domain crawl in one study, 57.6% of a 219-host panel of notable tech sites in another, which its own author calls a cohort figure rather than a web-wide one. Effectiveness is where it gets bleak. Reported findings include a study month in which 97% of llms.txt files received zero AI-crawler requests, a 37,894-domain comparison finding 6.8 citations with the file against 6.7 without, and Google stating plainly that it does not use it. We could not trace those figures to primary datasets, so hold them loosely — but the pattern they describe, of a publisher-side convention nobody queries, is the base rate this product is arguing against.

Why the asymmetry is still in your favour

And yet the honest read is that the risk shape here is better than it looks, precisely because of the billing direction. Work the two failure modes:

  • Nobody uses it. Your crawl runs, you pay ingestion and storage, and the query meter stays near zero. Cloudflare's own scheduled syncs pause after 31 days with no search requests, so even the ingestion bill decays on its own. Cost of being wrong: small and self-limiting.
  • Everybody uses it. You pay per query, on an unauthenticated endpoint, with no published cap. Cost of being right: unbounded until you bound it.

That is an unusual arrangement, and it inverts how teams normally evaluate a free beta. The reason to hesitate is not that this will fail. It is that you have no instrument for the version where it succeeds, and the instrument has to be in place before the thing you are hoping for happens.

A publisher monetising crawls Hold off, or scope the endpoint to a subset of paths You would be paying to serve the traffic you are elsewhere charging for, and the two products do not currently meet
Commercial, with a bot-blocking posture Check how your managed rules treat the path before enabling Bot management still applies; you can ship an endpoint your own rules refuse
Already running an MCP server or your own retrieval API Enable it as a second front door, not a replacement Same AI Search instance can be exposed via REST or MCP; the well-known path adds discoverability you do not control

In every row the first action is the same and it takes ten minutes: put a rate limit on /.well-known/search, set a billing alert on the AI Search meters, and query your own endpoint once to see how many results and how many tokens actually come back. Do that and the rest of this is upside.

FAQ

Does enabling Search for Agents let AI crawlers past my bot rules?

No. Cloudflare's announcement states that usage is subject to your domain settings, including bot management, and the crawl service is reported to honour robots.txt. The practical risk runs the other way: a zone with aggressive AI-bot blocking can advertise an endpoint its own rules then refuse.

Who pays when an agent queries my site?

You do. The three meters — ingestion, storage, and queries served — all run against the site owner's Cloudflare account, following AI Search pricing, with billing from 1 December 2026. The querying agent pays Cloudflare nothing to ask.

Is /.well-known/search a standard?

No. RFC 8615 standardises the /.well-known/ prefix and delegates the suffixes to an IANA registry, and search is not in the registry listings we could reach. It is a Cloudflare convention on a standard prefix, with no interoperability guarantee.

How many results does the endpoint return?

Unresolved. New instances are reported to default to ten chunks, while independent testers querying Cloudflare's own documentation instance report fifty results per query at roughly 10,000–12,000 tokens. Measure your own instance before budgeting context or spend.

Is this just AutoRAG with a new front door?

Substantially, yes. The toggle creates a normal Cloudflare AI Search instance — AI Search being the renamed AutoRAG — which you can also expose via REST or MCP, bind from a Worker, or use as a human-facing search box. What is new is the zero-configuration setup and the advertised well-known path.

Should I remove my llms.txt if I enable this?

No reason to. They do different jobs: one is a list of pages to read, the other an index to query, and Cloudflare publishes both on its own docs. The relevant comparison is cost, not conflict — serving a static file is free and serving queries is not.
