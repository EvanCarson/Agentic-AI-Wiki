Something fetched the secret URL in more than half of the prompts Dragos sent to one lab's models — and in roughly two-thirds of the ones flagged for zero data retention, usually two or three days after the request. Read that ordering again: the flagged prompts came back more often than the unflagged ones. The setting was not merely unreliable, it did not correlate with being honoured at all, and the only thing in this week's three disclosures that produced actual evidence was a planted canary rather than a contract.

At a glance

Three disclosures in four days, and they are one story read in the right order.

None of the three named the compromised providers, and Dragos named neither the three hosting providers nor the lab whose scraper it watched. That is frustrating and it is also beside the point: the finding that changes your engineering is not which vendor, it is that a privacy setting with no enforcement mechanism behaved, in this sample, exactly as if it did not exist.

What the canary measured that no audit can

A vendor questionnaire asks whether a provider retains your prompts. The provider answers no, in good faith, about the system it knows about. Both parties then file the answer and neither has learned anything, because the claim is unfalsifiable from the outside: a request you cannot observe after it leaves your process, processed by infrastructure you cannot inspect, deleted according to a schedule you cannot verify. Every control in that chain is a promise, and promises fail silently.

A canary converts the unfalsifiable claim into an observable event. Put a unique, never-used URL inside the prompt and the question stops being "do you retain this" and becomes "did anything, anywhere, ever fetch this". A fetch is positive evidence with a timestamp, a source address and a user agent. The absence of a fetch proves much less — plenty of retention never gets touched — but one fetch collapses the whole discussion, and that asymmetry is the entire value of the technique.

Which is why the headline number from the Dragos work is not the two million prompts. It is the two or three days. A scraper that requests a secret URL seventy-two hours after the inference request is not a logging artefact or a misconfigured proxy. It is a second system, reading a stored copy, on its own schedule — which means there was a store, it persisted across days, and something with a different job had access to it.

If you want one sentence to take to your own stack: a canary URL in your prompt costs nothing, requires no cooperation from your vendor, and is the only retention control you own that produces evidence rather than assurances. The mechanics are in honeytokens for agent systems, including why a token that triggers on use survives an agent stack and one that triggers on read does not.

The flag did not correlate

Take the lab finding on its own terms. Across July to October, a scraper belonging to an unnamed lab requested the canary URLs embedded in more than half of the prompts sent to its models. Of the prompts carrying an explicit zero-data-retention or no-data-collection flag, the figure was roughly two-thirds. Prompts routed to United States hosting providers that resell that lab's models set off the same scraper, which is the detail that should worry anyone who believed the reseller was a boundary.

Two readings are available and only one of them is comforting. The comforting one is that the flag was ignored — a bug, a pipeline that drops an unfamiliar header, a feature shipped before the plumbing. The uncomfortable one is that flagged traffic is selected traffic: a request someone bothered to mark as sensitive is, on average, a more interesting request, and a store that is being mined will be mined in the order of interestingness. The data in hand does not distinguish these, and you do not need it to, because both readings license the same action.

That action is not "stop using providers". It is to stop treating the flag as a control and start treating it as a preference. A control changes what the system can do; a preference changes what it intends to do. Zero data retention, on an endpoint nobody audits, is the second kind — and the wiki's longer argument about what that setting removes and never touched is in zero data retention and abuse monitoring, which already made the structural case that ZDR converges on "no routine retention" rather than "no retention". This week supplied the measurement that case was missing.

The retained corpus is a credential store nobody classified

Together AI's notice, published on 21 November and updated the following day, is the part of the week that has the cleanest causal chain, and it runs in a direction most threat models do not consider.

An AI coding agent working on a public repository committed a credential. Automated scanning caught it and the commit was reverted — the control worked, and it worked too slowly, because an unauthorised party had already taken the credential. What the credential opened was a private repository holding prompt samples the company had collected for routine testing. Those were downloaded. The intruder then searched the samples for credentials to other services and tested whether they still worked. Five of the credentials tested were canaries from the Dragos experiment, which is how the two disclosures are joined at the hip.

Follow the authority. A key leaked by an agent on a public repo did not give anyone access to Together AI's inference path; it gave them a few months of other companies' prompts, and prompts are where developers put API keys, connection strings, internal hostnames and the occasional customer record. The sample set was never classified as a secret store, because it was collected as test data — and test data is exactly the category that escapes retention schedules, access reviews and encryption requirements.

So the second-order lesson is a question to ask about your own systems, not about a vendor: which of your stores contains other people's secrets because somebody put them in a prompt? The eval set is one. The trace store is the big one. The fine-tune corpus is the one with no deletion story at all. The argument that your own retention is a larger exposure than the provider's has been on this site for a while; what was missing was a worked example of somebody's test bucket becoming somebody else's key list.

Note which control failed and which one held. Secret scanning on the repository detected the committed credential and the commit was reverted — and the credential was already gone. Detect-and-rotate loses a race it was never designed to run against an automated collector, which is the same conclusion Microsoft Threat Intelligence reached from the other direction in five minutes was the whole response window.

What OpenRouter's response does and does not tell you

On 22 November, OpenRouter off-boarded three inference providers, removed 125 endpoints from zero-data-retention eligibility, and notified potentially affected customers. It said it reviewed the findings with the researchers and the providers and found no breach of its own systems, and that restoring eligibility for any removed endpoint requires appropriate assurances and validation.

Three things in that are worth separating, because the number is the least informative part.

  • 125 is not a count of leaks. OpenRouter says the removals cover providers flagged by the researchers plus additional endpoints pulled as a precaution. A precautionary removal is the correct move and it is also an admission: the router could not tell, endpoint by endpoint, which ones were honouring the flag. If the intermediary cannot tell, you certainly cannot.
  • The eligibility was per endpoint, not per vendor. That is the right granularity and it is finer than almost any procurement process. Your vendor register has a row for a company; the retention property lives on a model-and-endpoint pair, and a router can move your traffic between those pairs within a single billing relationship.
  • No breach of the router is a narrow claim, correctly stated. The router was a conduit, not a victim, and the distinction matters for your incident write-up: nobody's gateway was compromised, and prompts still left through it. A pass-through architecture transfers data without taking custody of the risk, which is precisely what makes it attractive and precisely what makes it hard to assure.

The structural point is that the party best placed to detect this was none of the three. Not the customer, who cannot see past the API. Not the router, which proxies bytes it is not supposed to read. Not the provider, which in three cases did not know it had been compromised until Dragos told it. Detection came from outside the commercial chain entirely, from someone who planted a tracer and waited four months. That is a market failure with a cheap individual remedy, which is an unusual and lucky combination.

What to change this week

Four actions, in the order that the evidence supports rather than the order that feels proportionate.

  • Treat every secret that has transited third-party inference as disclosed, and revoke rather than rotate. "Rotate your keys" is the advice in every write-up and it is incomplete, because you cannot rotate what you cannot enumerate, and the attacker's copy lives somewhere you have no delete API for. Revoking the authority the credential carried is the only step whose completion you can verify — the enumeration problem and the revocation order are worked through in rotating a credential an agent has handled.
  • Stop sending secrets in prompts, structurally rather than by policy. A credential the model can read is a credential one crafted instruction away from a tool argument or a log line; the broker pattern that keeps it out of reach is in secrets management for agents. This is the control that would have made the Together AI sample set worthless to the intruder.
  • Pin the endpoint, not the vendor. If a router can select your serving endpoint, your retention property is whatever that endpoint's happens to be today. Maintain an allowlist and fail the request rather than silently taking an unapproved route — the failover path is the one that gets people, per graceful degradation and fallback, and the shared-dependency version of the same trap is in correlated failure across providers.
  • Plant your own canaries and leave them there. One unique URL per provider, per endpoint, per month, in a small sample of real traffic, with a collector that records the fetch. This is a weekend of work and it is the only line in your vendor-risk file that will ever contain evidence. The ongoing version — measuring susceptibility continuously instead of at audit time — is the injection-canary idea in the same page.

And one thing not to do: do not read this as an argument for self-hosting. A self-hosted model removes the third-party retention surface and replaces it with your own, which this week's evidence suggests is usually the larger one. The honest framing is that you have traded a store you cannot inspect for a store you can — and then have to.

FAQ

Does this mean zero data retention is worthless?

No. It means ZDR is a contractual commitment rather than a technical enforcement mechanism, so its value depends entirely on the counterparty and on your ability to detect a violation. Keep asking for it, keep it in the contract, and stop treating it as the control that lets you send sensitive content you would not otherwise send.

Should I rotate every API key that has ever appeared in a prompt?

Revoke them, which is stronger and more verifiable than rotating. If you cannot enumerate which keys appeared in prompts — most teams cannot — then the credentials that were long-lived and broadly scoped are the ones to retire first, and the structural fix is to stop issuing that kind.

Were any providers named?

Not the compromised ones. Dragos did not name the three hosting providers or the lab whose scraper it observed, and OpenRouter did not name the three providers it off-boarded. Together AI named itself in its own notice, which is a different kind of disclosure.

How do I plant a canary without breaking my own traffic?

Append a short, inert line to a small sample of prompts — a unique URL on a domain you control, framed as a reference the model has no reason to act on — and log every request to that domain with its source address and timestamp. Keep the sample small enough that it does not change your outputs and keep the URLs unique per endpoint so a fetch identifies the path.

Does a gateway or AI proxy protect me here?

It gives you one place to enforce an endpoint allowlist and one place to redact, which is genuinely useful. It does not change what happens after the request leaves it, and in a pass-through design the gateway is not in a position to know. Treat it as the place you implement the control, not as the control.
