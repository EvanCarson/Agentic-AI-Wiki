Attack success rate: three numbers are hiding in the one you were given.

Attack success rate is the share of adversarial attempts that achieve the attacker's goal — and on its own it is not a measurement, because the same system truthfully reports 0% and 78% depending on how many attempts the attacker gets. An ASR is only interpretable alongside three disclosures: the attempt budget, whether the attacker could adapt, and who decided what counted as success. Any of the three left out, and you are reading a number whose denominator somebody chose for you.

STEP 1

The attempt budget moves the number by two orders of magnitude.

In Best-of-N Jailbreaking (arXiv:2412.03556, NeurIPS 2025), the attack is almost insultingly simple: take a harmful request, shuffle its capitalisation and spelling, and send the variants until one lands. No gradients, no optimiser, no access to weights. With 10,000 sampled variations of HarmBench queries it reaches 78% ASR on Claude 3.5 Sonnet and 89% on GPT-4o. The unaugmented single-attempt baseline for Claude Sonnet is reported as zero — low enough that the paper's plot omits it.

Nothing about the model changed between those two figures. Only N did. The authors fit the relationship as -log(ASR) = aN^(-b) — a straight line in log-log space — and report that fitting the first 1,000 samples forecasts the 10,000-sample result within an order of magnitude. A 2026 follow-up (arXiv:2609.32116) argues the curve is not a fixed power law and that the apparent crossover may be an artefact of finite dataset size, which is a quarrel about the exponent rather than about the direction: more attempts, more success, without limit in any published measurement.

So "1% attack success rate" is not a claim about a system. It is a claim about a system and a number of tries, and the second half is usually missing. Ask for it before you ask anything else — and notice that a real attacker's budget is set by their patience and your rate limits, not by the evaluator's compute.

STEP 2

A static attack set measures yesterday's attacker.

The second disclosure is whether the attacker was allowed to respond to the defence. Almost all published defence evaluations replay a fixed corpus of known jailbreak or injection strings, which measures robustness against attacks that existed when the corpus was built.

In The Attacker Moves Second (arXiv:2510.09023, Nasr, Carlini et al., October 2025), the authors took twelve published defences and attacked them adaptively — gradient descent, reinforcement learning, random search and human-guided exploration — rather than replaying a corpus. They bypassed all twelve, most above 90% ASR, against the far lower rates the defences' own papers reported. The uncomfortable corollary they draw is that a defence advertising a very low number on a public benchmark is often among the easiest to break, because the low number is evidence the defence was tuned against that corpus.

The 2025 UK AISI × Gray Swan agent red-teaming competition (arXiv:2507.20526) shows both halves of this in one dataset, which is why it is the most useful single citation here. Roughly 1.8 million attempts from almost 2,000 participants against 22 frontier agents across 44 deployment scenarios produced more than 60,000 successful policy violations. Per-model ASR ran from 1.47% to 6.49%, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet (Thinking) most robust. Both of these are true: per attempt, these agents resisted roughly 95% of attacks, and nearly all agents violated policy on most behaviours within 10 to 100 queries. Which sentence you print decides whether your reader thinks the problem is solved.

STEP 3

Someone decided what counted, and the choice is worth tens of points.

The third disclosure is the success criterion. Many jailbreak evaluations score an attempt successful if the model did not explicitly refuse, which conflates compliance with capability. A StrongREJECT for Empty Jailbreaks (arXiv:2402.10260, NeurIPS 2024) worked the consequence through on a specific published result: translating forbidden requests into Scots Gaelic was reported to bypass GPT-4 at 43.08%. Reproducing it, the StrongREJECT authors found the outputs generally vacuous or incoherent, containing no actionable information. The same transcripts, two graders, 43% versus approximately nothing.

Their explanation is the part worth remembering: a jailbreak that defeats safety fine-tuning tends to degrade the model's capability along with its caution, so the response looks compliant and is useless. A grader that only checks for refusal cannot tell those apart, and will reward the attack for breaking the model.

For agents the criterion has more moving parts, because success is a state of the world rather than a string. AgentDojo (arXiv:2406.13352, NeurIPS 2024) is the canonical definer of the triple you actually want: benign utility (tasks completed with no attack), utility under attack, and targeted ASR (the injected goal actually executed, checked against final environment state). Reporting only the third lets a defence that cuts ASR to near zero by breaking the agent look like a win. Gray Swan's 2026 indirect-injection arena (arXiv:2603.15714) went further and required, for a success, that the agent also conceal the harmful action from the user — a stricter bar that mechanically lowers ASR relative to suites without it.

Three graders on the same transcripts give three ASRs. Before comparing two numbers, check they share a judge, a success definition and a deduplication rule — Gray Swan's arena, for instance, deduplicates attempts by hash per model-and-behaviour pair, which changes the denominator on its own.

STEP 4

What good reporting looks like, and why nothing forces it.

The best current practice is a vendor's, not a standard's. Anthropic's system cards report prompt-injection results on the Gray Swan benchmark as the probability an attacker finds a successful attack after k=1, k=10 and k=15 attempts — the budget is in the metric's name, so the curve rather than a point is what gets published. Two honest limitations ride along, and both are disclosed: the attacker is trained on these scenarios against earlier Claude models and then run unchanged, which makes it a non-adaptive ceiling in exactly the sense STEP 2 warns about; and Anthropic notes the earlier benchmark had nearly saturated and is being retired as a primary measure, which is what happens to any fixed corpus.

Contrast that with the same company's consumer-facing page for the same product class, which reports attack success rates below 0.08% against internal testing — no attempt budget, no attack-set size, no date. The earlier pilot post at least gave 123 test cases across 29 attack scenarios and a 23.6% rate without mitigations; a case count is not an attempt budget, but it is more than nothing. This is not special pleading against one vendor: it is the normal gradient between a document written to be checked and a document written to reassure, and you will find it everywhere.

No standard closes the gap. NIST AI 100-2 supplies the threat-model vocabulary — attacker goals, capabilities and knowledge — but it is a taxonomy, not a reporting requirement. MITRE ATLAS catalogues techniques without success rates. OWASP's red-teaming guidance structures engagements without mandating a budget disclosure. The OECD metrics catalogue defines ASR as successful attacks over total attempts and stops there. The research-side proposals exist — reporting ASR@B under a shared target-call budget, or its inverse as an attack-resistance rate at k attempts, which forces k into the number by construction — and none of them is binding on anyone.

So the burden is on the reader. Three questions, asked in order: how many attempts, could the attacker adapt, who graded it and against what. A number that cannot answer all three is marketing, however technical the document it appears in.

When you report your own: publish the curve, not the point — ASR at 1, 10 and 100 attempts, since the shape is the finding. State the attack set, its size, its date, and whether the attacker adapted. Name the judge and show your non-refusal rate alongside, so a defence that works by lobotomising the agent cannot hide. And treat your own number as a floor rather than a bound, because every published measurement so far rises with the budget — the honest claim is "we resisted this attacker at this budget", never "our ASR is 1%".
