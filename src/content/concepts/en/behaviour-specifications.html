Behaviour specifications.

A revision that deletes a rule cannot turn your test suite red, and that is how most teams will discover their model's published behaviour moved. Two frontier labs now publish dated, diffable specifications of what their models are trained to do — Anthropic's constitution under CC0, OpenAI's Model Spec at a versioned URL with a changelog. Pin a version, diff it on every release, and read the removals rather than the additions.

STEP 1

A behaviour spec states what the model is supposed to do, which is a different document from what it did.

Three kinds of vendor document get confused with each other, and only one of them is a specification.

  • A behaviour specification states the rules the model is trained and evaluated against: who it takes instructions from, what it refuses, how it resolves a conflict between your instruction and a user's. It is normative and it is about the model family, not a checkpoint.
  • A system card is an empirical safety-testing record about one checkpoint under one elicitation methodology. It is descriptive, and it expires.
  • API documentation describes the interface. It says nothing about behaviour under conflict, which is exactly what an agent spends its day in.

The distinction matters because only the specification answers the question an agent builder actually has: when my system prompt tells the model to do something and its training tells it not to, which wins, and is that answer stable? Anthropic published Claude's Constitution in full in January 2026, released under CC0 1.0 — the public-domain dedication, so you may quote it into your own policy documents without a licence question. OpenAI's Model Spec has been published since 2024 and now lives at dated URLs (the current revision is model-spec.openai.com/2026-10-29.html) with a changelog in the openai/model_spec repository. Google publishes safety frameworks and model cards but, as of November 2026, no equivalent normative behaviour document.

A spec is a statement of intent by the lab, not a guarantee about the weights you are served. It tells you what the post-training was aiming at. Treat the gap between the spec and the behaviour as your own measurement problem — that is what an acceptance eval is for.

STEP 2

The priority order is the part that binds you, and helpfulness is not first.

Both published specs are structured as a small set of values with an explicit conflict-resolution rule, and that rule is the load-bearing sentence for anyone writing a system prompt.

Anthropic's constitution names four properties for Claude models — broad safety, broad ethics, compliance with Anthropic's guidelines, and genuine helpfulness — and says that where they conflict Claude should generally prioritise them in that order. Read the ordering as a product constraint. Helpfulness to the operator and the user is the fourth of four. An instruction that trades a guideline for a better outcome in your product loses by published design, not by accident, and no amount of prompt engineering is supposed to reverse that. The hedge in "generally" is doing real work too: it is a statement that the order is a default under judgment rather than a lexicographic rule, which is why two superficially similar refusals can resolve differently.

OpenAI's October 2026 revision reorganised the Model Spec around five values — faithfulness to human intent, minimising harm, honesty and transparency, human agency, and care — and states that those values guide two core tensions: individual against collective interests, and immediate instructions against longer-term goals. The second tension is the agentic one. A long-running agent is, by construction, a machine that has been given immediate instructions and is expected to serve a longer-term goal, and the spec is telling you that the resolution is a judgment the model makes rather than a flag you set.

Underneath both sits the instruction hierarchy — platform above operator above user above tool output. The hierarchy tells you whose instruction wins; the value ordering tells you what happens when the winner's instruction conflicts with the model's training. Builders routinely plan for the first and get surprised by the second.

STEP 3

It is a versioned dependency, and the dangerous edit is a deletion.

A spec with a dated URL and a changelog is a dependency you can pin, which also means it is a dependency that changes without your release. OpenAI shipped at least two revisions in 2026 — version 2026.08.18 and then the 29 October rewrite, which went as far as renaming the document's subject from "the assistant" to "GPT". Secondary coverage of that rewrite also reports that much of the earlier detailed style guidance was cut; we have not traced that to the changelog, so treat the rename as the verified example and the cut as the thing to check in the diff yourself.

Now consider how each kind of edit reaches you:

# how a spec revision shows up in your test suite
edit type              example                         your evals
"added rule"           new teen-interaction guidance   may go red   ← you notice
"tightened rule"       narrower refusal boundary       may go red   ← you notice
"clarified wording"    same behaviour, new prose       stays green  ← harmless
"removed rule"         style guidance deleted          stays green  ← you never notice
# a deleted constraint widens the set of outputs that pass. nothing fails.

This asymmetry is the whole practical point. Your eval suite is a set of assertions written against the behaviour you observed, which was shaped by the spec in force when you wrote them. A tightening breaks an assertion and pages you. A removal does not: the behaviour that used to be required becomes merely permitted, the old output still passes, and a new output you never thought to forbid also passes. If your product leaned on a style rule the lab has stopped specifying — a hedging convention, a refusal phrasing your parser keys on, a default to asking a clarifying question — you now depend on a behaviour nobody promises and your tests will not tell you when it drifts.

The same logic runs through refusals: a spec revision that relaxes a boundary does not break a single test in a suite built around that boundary holding. It is visible only in production refusal rates, which is why monitoring refusals as a time series catches spec drift that offline evals structurally cannot.

STEP 4

Pin it, diff it, and keep your own acceptance eval for everything it does not say.

Three cheap practices, in order of how much they buy:

  • Record the spec version next to the model version. Your deployment metadata already names a model snapshot; add the spec revision you validated against. When behaviour changes, the first question is which of the two moved, and without the second field that question takes a week. This is the same discipline as pinning and verification applied to a document instead of a digest.
  • Diff on revision, and read the deletions first. For OpenAI this is a git diff against a public repository; for Anthropic's CC0 document it is a file you are explicitly permitted to keep a copy of. Budget twenty minutes. You are looking for removed constraints, loosened boundaries, and changed conflict-resolution language — not new prose.
  • Encode the behaviour you depend on, not the behaviour you were promised. Anything your parser, your UI or your guardrail assumes about model output belongs in an assertion you own, whether or not the spec currently requires it. A spec is a reason to expect a behaviour; an eval is the only thing that notices when the expectation stops being true. This is the same argument as keeping a pinned acceptance suite alongside a system card.

What a spec genuinely buys you is harder to get any other way: it is citable. When a reviewer, a customer or an auditor asks why the agent refused, "the vendor's published specification, revision 2026-10-29, section on harm minimisation" is an answer with a URL. An appeal to observed behaviour is not. That makes the spec the best available input to your own policy documents and the worst available substitute for testing.

Do this once, this week: add a spec_revision field to wherever you record the model you deploy, populate it with the dated spec you last read, and put a calendar reminder to diff it. The whole value is in the second half — the field makes "which moved, the model or the rules?" a lookup instead of an investigation, and the diff is the only place a deleted constraint is ever visible. Then write down the three behaviours your product would break without, and make each one an assertion you own.
