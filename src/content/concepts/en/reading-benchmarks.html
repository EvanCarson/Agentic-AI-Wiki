Evaluation & leaderboards: reading benchmarks critically.

A benchmark score is a reading from a measuring instrument, and you should only trust it once you've checked the instrument. Three checks cover it. What does this instrument actually measure? Is it still sound, or has it leaked into training data and accumulated broken tests? And is the gap you're looking at bigger than the instrument's own noise? This entry covers the benchmarks agent builders actually run into in 2026, how the best-known one fell apart, the arithmetic of noise, and why your own small eval set is the only instrument aimed at your task.

STEP 1

The three kinds of evaluation you will see.

  • Static benchmark suites. Fixed tasks with known answers or executable checks: SWE-bench Verified and SWE-bench Pro for code, Humanity's Last Exam for expert knowledge, GAIA for tool-using assistants. They're reproducible and comparable. They're also fixed, which makes them gameable, prone to leaking into training data, and only as good as their graders.
  • Human-preference arenas. LMArena (formerly Chatbot Arena) shows users two anonymous answers and asks them to vote, then turns the votes into ratings. It captures real-world preference well, but what it measures is "which answer people liked," not "which answer was correct."
  • Task-specific / private evals. Your own dataset, scored against your own success criteria. They're the least general kind and the most useful for a decision, because they're the only kind that measures the thing you care about.

Each kind answers a different question. The most common evaluation error is treating "tops the arena" as if it meant "most correct on my extraction task." For agents there's a fourth variable on top of the model: the harness. A SWE-bench or Terminal-Bench number belongs to a model running in a particular scaffold, with particular tools, a particular turn budget and a particular number of attempts. Put the same model in your loop and you get a different number.

STEP 2

A field guide to the benchmarks agent builders see.

Before you weigh any score, find out what its benchmark measures and where it misleads. Details are as of October 2026.

Benchmark
What it measures
Read it with
SWE-bench Verified
500 human-validated GitHub issues (released August 2024). The agent writes a patch and hidden tests decide whether it passes.
OpenAI stopped reporting it in February 2026 because of flawed tests and contamination (STEP 3). Treat any score on it as a historical figure.
SWE-bench Pro
Scale AI's harder, longer-horizon successor. Its public split has 731 tasks.
In July 2026 OpenAI estimated that about 30% of the public tasks were broken and withdrew its earlier recommendation of the benchmark.
Terminal-Bench
Hard, realistic command-line tasks. Each task has its own environment and verification tests. Version 2.0 had 89 tasks (ICLR 2026 paper), and tbench.ai now runs a 4.0 leaderboard.
This is closer to real agent work than single-patch coding. Scores are reported per agent and model pair, so the harness is part of the number.
τ-bench / τ²-bench
A customer-service agent talks to a simulated user while following a policy and calling APIs (retail, airline). τ²-bench adds a telecom domain in which the user also acts through tools.
Look for its pass^k reliability metric (STEP 5). Agents score noticeably lower once they have to guide a user who acts as well.
GAIA
466 real-world assistant questions that need browsing, file handling and multi-step tool use. Each has a short answer that's unambiguous to check.
The launch paper reported humans at 92% and GPT-4 with plugins at 15%. It's a good test of tool use, but it's old and public, so assume leakage.
Humanity's Last Exam
2,500 expert-written, auto-gradable questions across dozens of subjects, built to resist internet lookup.
It measures frontier academic knowledge and calibration, not agency. A high HLE score tells you little about whether a model can run a loop.
METR time horizon
The length of task, measured in human expert time, that a model completes with 50% success. In the March 2025 paper it was doubling roughly every seven months.
This is the best available single trend line for autonomy. Note that it's set at 50% success, which is far below what production needs.
LMArena
Crowd pairwise votes turned into Bradley-Terry ratings.
Check whether style control is on. When it was introduced in August 2024 it moved models by up to a dozen ranks.

Style control deserves a closer look, because it shows the arena bias is real and measurable. LMArena added answer length and markdown headers, bold text and lists as covariates in its rating model. Answer length turned out to be the strongest style factor. Once style was controlled for, grok-2-mini fell from 6th to 18th overall, gpt-4o-mini fell from 6th to 11th, and claude-3-opus rose from 16th to 10th. A rank you read without that adjustment partly reflects formatting.

STEP 3

Case study: how the standard coding benchmark wore out.

OpenAI introduced SWE-bench Verified in August 2024 to fix the noisy original SWE-bench, and for eighteen months it was the coding-agent number. In February 2026 OpenAI published "Why we no longer evaluate SWE-bench Verified." It had audited 138 of the problems its own model failed to solve consistently, with each one reviewed by experienced engineers, and found that 59.4% had material problems in their tests or problem statements. Many of those were tests that rejected functionally correct fixes. It also found that frontier models from three vendors could reproduce some of the original fixes from memory. Meanwhile the top score had moved only from 74.9% to 80.9% in six months, so it was no longer possible to tell model limits apart from dataset defects.

OpenAI pointed people to SWE-bench Pro instead. Five months later, in July 2026, an OpenAI audit of Pro's 731 public tasks put roughly 30% of them as broken. An automated pipeline flagged 27.4% and human annotators flagged 34.1%. OpenAI withdrew the recommendation. Over eight months, frontier pass rates on that split had risen from 23.3% to 80.3%.

The lesson isn't that one benchmark was bad. Every public benchmark decays. It leaks into training data, the field optimizes against it, and its graders' mistakes start to dominate the remaining headroom. Once a score is near the ceiling, the gap between two models can come as much from the broken tasks as from the models.

STEP 4

The noise math: what difference can you actually detect?

Even with a sound, uncontaminated benchmark, a score is an estimate taken from a finite sample. With n items and measured accuracy p, the standard error is √(p(1−p)/n), and a 95% confidence interval is about ±1.96 standard errors. When you compare two models that were scored independently, the uncertainty on the difference is about √2 times larger:

# 95% half-widths, in accuracy points, at p = 0.80
n items   one score   gap between two models
   50      ±11.1         ±15.7
  100       ±7.8         ±11.1
  200       ±5.5          ±7.8
  500       ±3.5          ±5.0     # SWE-bench Verified size
 2500       ±1.6          ±2.2     # Humanity's Last Exam size

This is why "Model A beats Model B by 1.5 points" on a 500-item benchmark usually means nothing. It's also why your 50-example eval can't settle a 5-point question. The table actually understates the noise for agents, because it treats each item as a fixed coin, while an agent run is itself random: the same model on the same task passes on one run and fails on the next. Two cheap fixes help. Score both models on the same items and compare them pair by pair, which removes the between-item variance. And run each item several times, which is what the next step is about.

STEP 5

pass@k vs pass^k: discovery vs reliability.

The τ-bench paper (Sierra, 2024) made a distinction that every agent builder should keep in mind:

  • pass@k is the chance that at least one of k independent attempts succeeds. It rises with k. This is the right metric when a verifier can choose the winner, as with test-gated code generation.
  • pass^k is the chance that all k attempts succeed. It falls with k, and it's the right metric when every user interaction counts, as with an agent in front of customers.

The difference is large. On a task that succeeds 80% of the time on independent runs, pass@8 is about 100%, while pass^8 is 0.88 ≈ 17%. In the paper itself, GPT-4o's function-calling agent scored 61.2% pass^1 on τ-bench retail, and pass^8 fell below 25%. A headline single-run score tells you whether the model can do the task. It tells you nothing about whether it does the task every time, and for most production agents that second question is the one that matters. Vendor tables that report "best of k" or pass@k for an agent are answering the easier question.

STEP 6

How to actually use them.

Public benchmarks are good for one job: shortlisting. Pick the one or two from STEP 2 that look most like your workload, and use them to choose a handful of candidate models in the right capability class. After that, switch to an instrument you control:

  • Collect 100–200 examples that look like your real traffic, including the hard and weird cases. Use the table in STEP 4 to know beforehand which gaps the set can resolve. At 200 items, anything under about 8 points between independently scored models is noise.
  • Define a concrete, automatable success metric for your task, not a generic score.
  • Run every shortlisted model on the same items, several times each, and report the paired difference and pass^k alongside the mean.
  • Record cost and latency in the same run, so the comparison covers the whole cost / quality / latency triangle.

This set is the most valuable artifact you'll build in this whole topic. It outlives every model release. It's also private, so nobody trains on it and it doesn't decay the way SWE-bench Verified did. And it's the only thing that reliably answers "is the new model better for me?" See evals 101 and agent evaluation for how to build one.

STEP 7

Staying current without thrashing.

Rankings reset every few months, and the benchmarks themselves get retired, so chasing every launch is wasted effort. The stable practice is to keep your eval set under version control and hide the model behind a thin seam. When a credible new release appears, run your eval, compare on all three axes, and switch only when the improvement is real, outside the noise band, and matters for your workload. Follow the field through structural shifts, not leaderboard reshuffles. Structural shifts include a new modality, a large price move, a time-horizon trend that changes what you can delegate, or the news that a benchmark you relied on has been retired. That way you stay current on whatever changes your options without re-deciding every week.
